Hard Lines

Seven lines that don’t get renegotiated. Each one is an if-then that fires at the exact moment you usually lose, under one belief you already hold.

The Belief
I give 100%.
I’m not weak.
In the moment, say it by name: “Sean, you give 100%.”
Why this works for youYOUR RECORD
The belief was already there while you were still drinking. Drinking survived beside it because there was room to argue: “it doesn’t really affect me.”
The sleep numbers closed that room. Once the evidence said it cost you, carrying on meant knowingly choosing weakness, and the belief won’t allow that.
So a line holds when two things are true: it is exact (no grey zone to bargain in) and the evidence is in front of you. The Log tab is the second half. It is not optional.
Graded as your own record, not research: it is one case, and it is yours. The general idea that people judge who they are from what they watch themselves do is self-perception theory (Bem), which is well supported.
Aim it at the choice, never at yourself. When a line breaks, the verdict is “that choice was weak,” and the next choice is the one that counts. Turned on yourself, the same belief becomes self-contempt, and self-criticism after a lapse tends to make the next lapse more likely, not less.
The Seven Lines
01EdiblesBC
What it buys you is switching off. The line has to buy that too, or the evening wins.
If I’m reaching for one, then I say the line and do the shutdown: tomorrow’s open work items on paper so they’re out of my head, then one switch-off thing — hot shower, audiobook, walk.
Same routine as line 03. Night prep and the shutdown are one ritual, not two.
Read your sleep and HRV trend after the first week, not the first few nights. A few nights of worse sleep and vivid dreams are common when regular cannabis use stops, and they would tell you a false story.
Grades: mental detachment from work after hours is consistently linked to better recovery and well-being B — Sonnentag’s research line and meta-analyses, mostly correlational. Writing tomorrow’s to-do list at bedtime helped people fall asleep faster than writing about finished tasks C — one small study (Scullin et al., 2018, 57 people). Sleep disruption during cannabis withdrawal B — part of the recognized withdrawal syndrome.
02The GymB
The gym is across the hall. The decision happens at the door of Room 148.
If I’m about to skip, then I walk across the hall and do one set.
One set counts as kept. The floor is set so low that you can’t fail it, which is what keeps the line unbroken.
Most days, one set turns into the session. On the days it doesn’t, the line still holds, and the line is what you’re building.
Grade: if-then plans (implementation intentions) improve goal attainment with a medium-to-large effect across many studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006 meta-analysis) B. The one-set floor itself is reasoning, not a tested claim.
03The Night BeforeB
A plan with no fixed trigger floats. Tie it to something you never skip.
If I’m heading to bed, then tomorrow gets laid out before I brush my teeth.
Paper list of open work (from line 01), clothes, gym bag, whatever tomorrow needs. Done before the teeth, never after.
Grade: same if-then evidence as line 02 B. Anchoring to an existing habit is how the trigger becomes automatic.
04TemperC
The line is not “don’t feel it.” It is “don’t speak from it.”
If I feel the heat rising — jaw, voice, chest — then I stop talking and take one long exhale before I say anything.
Kept means you caught it and paused, even if you were already annoyed. Broke means you spoke from the heat.
If it happened with the girls, go back first and repair, same day. Short, no “but.”
The breath itself lives in The Resilience Protocol, Pillar 02. Repair lives in The Father Protocol, Base Layer 4. Neither is repeated here.
Grade: the physiological sigh rests on one solid RCT (Balban et al., 2023) C. Resilience grades it “moderate”; on this page’s scale one trial is a C.
05Negative ThoughtsC
You don’t have to win the argument with a thought. You just have to stop obeying it.
If I catch a spiral, then I label it — “that’s a thought, not a fact” — and ask what 100% looks like in the next ten minutes.
The label creates distance. The question turns the energy into one action.
This is the same grammar as “not relevant,” which already works for you: concede the thought exists, deny it authority.
Grade: thought-labelling (cognitive defusion, from ACT) reduces how believable and distressing a thought feels in lab studies, with modest effects; the full ACT package has broader support C.
06Too Tired for the GirlsB
Ten real minutes beat an hour of half-there.
If I’m too tired to engage, then I give ten fully present minutes, phone in another room. After that, rest is earned.
The floor is ten minutes, not the whole evening. Being tired doesn’t break the line. Skipping the ten does.
What to do and say inside those ten minutes is in The Father Protocol. The phone-out-of-the-room rule is its Base Layer 2.
Grade: carried from Father — parental phone use during time with children is consistently linked to worse outcomes, mostly cross-sectional B. The ten-minute floor is reasoning.
07Self-EvidenceBREASONING
Two hours a week during term. The line guards those two hours, and the nerve to ship.
If my building time comes and I want to put it off, then I say the line, open the file, and do one concrete piece, even for 15 minutes.
If I’m holding back from putting something out there, then I ask “is holding back 100%?” and ship the smallest public piece.
The company is this page’s first test case: an identity line, measured with your own data, is the method you are selling.
Grade: the first trigger is the if-then evidence again B. “Ship the smallest public piece” is reasoning.
Making the belief louder
If-then at the moment of loss B — the seven lines above. This does most of the work.
Evidence that ends the argument YOUR RECORD — the Log tab. Without data, a tired mind talks its way around any slogan.
Values writing, now and then B — write a paragraph on why giving 100% matters: the girls, your students, your own standard. Self-affirmation makes people more open to evidence about their own behaviour; some effects have shrunk in replication.
Your name, not “I” C — “Sean, you give 100%” lets you see yourself from a coach’s angle rather than from inside the craving (Kross, distanced self-talk).
Not daily chanting. Repeating positive statements has a mixed record; in one study it made people with low self-esteem feel worse (Wood et al., 2009).
The One Thing
“Is this 100%?”
Evidence key
A strong, replicated · B good, consistent, often correlational or with caveats · C limited: one trial, small studies or lab effects · D weak · REASONING / YOUR RECORD not graded on the research scale, and never dressed up as if it were.

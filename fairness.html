The Fairness Problem

“It’s not fair” and “Why hasn’t anyone noticed?” are two faces of one belief, and they get equal weight here. CBT’s answer is not to argue with the belief but to test it against your own evidence until it stops steering. Four tools in the Work tab.

The belief
One rule underneath both. “If I do the right thing, the world will register it and pay it back.” Track A is what you feel when the rule is broken: it’s not fair. Track B is what you do while you wait for the rule to be honoured: you wait to be noticed. Same rule, one reaction and one behaviour. Both get worked on, equally.
A“It’s not fair”C
CBT calls it the fallacy of fairness: measuring events against what should happen, then resenting the gap.
It shows up as surprise, then resentment. Something went against you, and it feels less like bad luck than like a broken agreement. The surprise is the useful part. It means your model predicted one thing and the world did another.
Its sentences: “That shouldn’t have happened.” “After everything I did.” “I don’t deserve this.”
Grade C. It is a standard entry in CBT distortion lists, but there is almost no trial evidence on this one distortion on its own. It is treated inside general cognitive restructuring.
B“Why hasn’t anyone noticed?”C
CBT calls it the heaven’s reward fallacy: the belief that good work will be repaid without you doing anything more.
This track is quieter than A, and it costs more. It doesn’t feel like anything. It feels like patience. You do the work well and then you leave the last step to someone else: showing it, asking, following up, putting it in front of the right person.
Its sentences: “They should see it.” “If it’s good it’ll get picked up.” “I’m not going to blow my own horn.”
Grade C, for the same reason as A. Listed in CBT distortion handouts, not studied on its own.
Why you can’t just drop itB
The belief is doing a job.
Research on belief in a just world separates two versions: the world is fair to me (personal) and the world is fair in general (general). In questionnaire studies, the personal version predicts wellbeing and self-esteem. The general version mostly doesn’t.
So the aim is not cynicism. “Nothing is ever fair” is the same error turned upside down. It is still a forecast made from a moral rule instead of from evidence.
What you keep: fairness as your own standard for how you behave. What you drop: fairness as a forecast of how the world will respond.
Grade B. Dalbert (1999) and several later replications across countries. These are correlations from questionnaires, not experiments, so they show the belief travels with wellbeing, not that it causes it.
What this page doesn’t cover. The in-the-moment handle (“Nothing is owed”) and the three tiers of hurt belong to The Grievance Protocol. The distancing move belongs to Resilience, Pillar 04. This page covers the slower job: testing the belief over weeks until your own record outweighs it. Same scope as the Grievance Protocol: strangers, work and the world. Not your children, where obligations are real.
Why test instead of argue
Head–heart lagB
You already know the world isn’t fair. That knowledge has never stopped the feeling.
CBT therapists call this head–heart lag: you hold the right belief as a sentence while acting on the old one. Reasoning with yourself reaches the sentence. A behavioural experiment reaches the expectation, because it puts a prediction in front of reality and you watch which one wins.
An experiment is a specific prediction from the old belief, an action that tests it, and an honest record of what happened.
In one direct comparison, people rated experiments as more powerful and compelling than thought records for changing a belief. C That is one study and self-reported ratings, so it supports putting experiments at the centre, not dropping thought records.
Grade B for experiments as a core CBT tool. The Oxford and King’s College groups have built thirty years of anxiety treatment around them. Grade C for experiments beating thought records head-to-head.
Why this suits you. Evidence that leaves no room to argue is the lever that has worked for you before. You also already run action research on yourself. This is the same method aimed at one belief: predict, act, record, compare.
The six steps
01Write the rule downB
A belief you can’t state can’t be tested.
CBT writes underlying beliefs as if–then rules, because an if–then makes a prediction and a prediction can fail.
Rule A: “If I play fair, I’ll be treated fairly.”
Rule B: “If my work is good, the right people will notice it.”
Rate how true each one feels right now, 0 to 100. Not how true you think it is, but how strongly it runs. Log it in Work, tool 1. That number is your baseline.
TipRewrite them in your own words if these don’t sound like the voice in your head. The test only works on the rule you actually run.
02Catch itB
A two-line record, written while it’s fresh. No analysis.
The tells: surprise followed by resentment · a “should” right before a decision · finishing something good and putting it away · waiting for someone to offer instead of asking.
Line 1, the situation: what a camera would have seen.
Line 2, the sentence: the words in your head, quoted exactly. Then how strong the feeling was, 0 to 10, and which track.
That’s it. A short record you actually keep beats a full seven-column record you skip.
Room 148A colleague’s unit gets mentioned at the staff meeting. Yours, built over the summer, doesn’t. Line 2: “I did twice the work and nobody even knows it exists.” B, 6.
StrangersSomeone cuts in at the Superstore checkout and the cashier serves them. Line 2: “Unbelievable.” A, 5.
AdminA last-minute request arrives after you’ve finished the thing it changes. Line 2: “I always get mine in on time and this is what I get.” A, 7.
IncomeYou finish a strong Self-Evidence chapter and close the file. Line 2: nothing, which is the tell. B, 0. Log the zero too.
03Swap the demand for a preferenceB
Keep wanting it. Stop requiring it.
This is the REBT move, and the Grievance Protocol covers it in full (Tier Two). Here it gets one line per track, used at the end of a catch:
A: “I’d prefer this had gone fairly. It didn’t have to. What does it change about what I do next?”
B: “I’d like this noticed. It won’t be unless I show it. Showing it is the last step of the job.”
Grade B. A meta-analysis of fifty years of REBT found medium effects on outcomes (d = 0.58) and on irrational beliefs themselves (d = 0.70). The evidence is for the whole package, not this one move tested alone.
04Run one experiment a weekB
This is the core of the page. Everything else feeds it.
Name the rule you’re testing, A or B.
Write the prediction first, specific enough to be wrong. Not “it won’t go well” but “she won’t reply within two days” or “none of the three will have heard of it.”
Rate the rule 0 to 100 before you act.
Do it cleanly. No hedging, no apologising in advance, no burying the ask. A hedge is a safety behaviour: it lets you write off the result whichever way it goes.
Record what happened, compare it with the prediction, and re-rate the rule.
Design rules from the clinical guidance (Oxford / King’s College group, 2025): test one specific belief, make the prediction concrete, drop safety behaviours, and review afterward what the result means for the belief. Small and real beats big and imagined.
Track A experiments · “it’s not fair”
ForecastBefore five situations this week, write two forecasts each: the fair one and the realistic one, based on what usually happens. Afterward, score which forecast was closer more often.
TrafficPrediction: “Bad drivers pick on me.” Count every cut-off on your commute for a week and note whether it was aimed at you or just happened near you.
AdminPrediction: “If I push back on a late request, it’ll go badly.” Once, reply politely with a real constraint and a counter-offer. Record what actually came back.
SurveyAsk two people you trust, separately: “Do you think doing things the right way gets you treated fairly at work?” Predict their answers first. Asking other people what they believe is a recognized form of behavioural experiment.
StrangersNext time someone jumps a line, predict how long the feeling will last if you do nothing, and how long it will last if you say one calm sentence. Then try one and time it.
Track B experiments · waiting to be noticed
AdminPrediction: “Telling the VP about a result will look like bragging, or get no reply.” Send two lines about one concrete student result with a number in it. Record the reply and how long it took.
Room 148Prediction: “People already know about the work I do.” Ask three colleagues whether they knew about one thing you built. Predict how many will say yes before you ask.
IncomePrediction: “Nobody will care about a Self-Evidence excerpt.” Put one short piece in front of real readers. Predict the response count first, then compare.
The askPick one thing you’ve been waiting to be offered: a timetable preference, a resource, a chance to present. Ask for it directly. Prediction: “They’ll say no.” Record the answer.
Night classPrediction: “They don’t notice the extra prep.” Ask the class for one line each on what helped most this term. Count how many name something you built.
UnseenFor one week, do your best work and tell nobody. At the end, count how much of it anyone mentioned. This tests Rule B directly, with no action from you to blame.
05Lock it in with if–then plansA
Decide the response before the trigger arrives.
An if–then plan ties a specific cue to a specific action. Across 94 independent tests, these plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65). They work best when the goal behind them is strong, so write them for the experiments you actually mean to run.
B: “If I finish something I’m proud of, then within 24 hours I show it to one person who matters for it.”
B: “If I catch myself waiting to be offered something, then I ask for it that day.”
A: “If ‘that’s not fair’ fires, then I write the two lines and ask what it changes about my next move.”
A: “If a late request lands, then I answer it on its merits, not on the balance of the account.”
Grade A for the meta-analysis itself (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). Using it on this particular belief is reasoning.
06Review weeklyD
Ten minutes. The chart does the arguing.
Re-rate both rules in tool 1. Watch the two lines, not any single week.
Read the closed experiments. One question: how often did the prediction from the old rule come true?
Choose next week’s experiment, alternating tracks so both get equal work.
Grade D. A weekly review is ordinary CBT practice, but its structure here is my design, not a tested protocol.
Six weeks
1
Week 1: rate both rules, then catch only. Aim for at least five records, split across both tracks. No experiments yet; you’re learning where it fires.
2
Week 2: first Track B experiment, the smallest one on the list. Write two if–then plans.
3
Week 3: first Track A experiment. Keep catching.
4
Week 4: a bigger Track B experiment, one where the result matters to you.
5
Week 5: a bigger Track A experiment.
6
Week 6: read the whole log. Compare both ratings with week 1. Decide which track needs another round.
How it goes wrong
Arguing instead of testing. If a session is all reasoning and no action, it has drifted back into the head.
Vague predictions. “It won’t go well” can’t fail. If it can’t fail, it can’t teach you anything.
Hedging the action. An ask buried in apologies, or a result shared “in case it’s useful.” The hedge gives you an excuse whichever way it goes.
Counting only the hits. Log the experiments where the old rule was right, too. Sometimes it will be, and that’s information, not failure.
Swinging into cynicism. “Nothing is ever fair” is the same forecast error turned upside down.
Running it at home. Out of scope. Obligations to your children are real.
The honest limit. CBT’s evidence comes mostly from clinical anxiety and depression. Using it on a fairness belief in a healthy adult is reasoning, not tested. Self-guided programs also run weaker than guided ones; one meta-analysis of self-guided stress programs found a small effect (g = 0.19). What makes up for that is that you’re not relying on the studies: your own log is the evidence.
The one thing
“What did I predict, and what actually happened?” Both tracks run on a forecast. That question turns each one into data.
Evidence key
A Meta-analytic, replicated.   B Solid but narrower: one strong meta-analysis, or a well-established clinical method.   C Thin: catalogued, plausible, few direct tests.   D My design, from reasoning.

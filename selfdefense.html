The Self-Defense Protocol

Everyone home safe, and no criminal liability — the survival move and the legal move are almost always the same move. Avoid → de-escalate → escape. Force is the last branch, and it ends the instant you can leave.

The legal floor · BC
Reasonable force, then stop
Canada has no “stand your ground.” s.34 is lawful only if all three hold: you reasonably believe force/threat is on you or another; you act to defend; the act is reasonable in the circumstances.
Proportionality is the whole game — the force needed to stop the threat, and it stops the instant the threat ends or you can escape. Continued force past that point turns you into the aggressor.
Defending family/friends is inside s.34 — covered, not extra legal risk.
Your training raises the bar — a court weighs your Krav skill (R v Khill). A reasonable trained person controls with less. Skill is a reason for restraint, not a licence.
Weapons trap: you cannot carry anything for self-defense. Pepper spray is a prohibited weapon; a knife or bear spray carried to defend yourself is an offence. The lawful answer to a known threat is police + a peace bond, never a blade in your pocket.
Read the threat · pre-assault cues
Two or more stacking → move
Hidden or busy hands — a waistband “check” is a weapon tell.
Closing distance after you already stepped back — bad intent.
Blading / loading — turning side-on, chin drop, weight to the back foot.
The “interview” — “You got a problem?” Designed to freeze you while he sets up. Answer the situation, not the question.
Sudden calm / target stare after agitation — the decision is made; the attack is next.
Multiple attackers: watch for flanking — one talks from the front while others drift to your sides. Never let yourself be surrounded, never go to the ground, just leave.
Map what you see to a pre-decided response — that is the orientation, done while calm.
The two postures
AProtecting family
You often can’t retreat freely. You are a barrier and an extractor, not a fighter.
Command word — a pre-agreed phrase = go now, to the rally point. Decide the word and the point today. Highest-leverage item on this page.
Interpose + loud boundary — body between threat and them; “Back up. Stay back.” Creates space and witnesses.
Extract as a unit — you between threat and family, moving to the exit.
Force only if the gap closes — burst until he stops, then resume extraction. Never pursue.
BSelf or friends
Escape wins on every axis — survival, legal, reputational. Ego is the enemy; a fight you could have left is a loss.
Leave — early, before it sets. The whole strategy most of the time.
De-escalate — open hands (a guard that also reads as calm to witnesses), even voice, give him a face-saving exit.
Boundary + distance — “I don’t want trouble — back up.”
Force only if forced — burst until he no longer can or will fight, then stop, leave, and you call police first.
Left is the mistake — standing still while they spread around you. Right is the fix — move so they stack on one line (nearest attacker between you and the rest), your body between threat and family, driving to the exit.
When escape is gone · respond & train
The bursts and footwork for every situation below are drilled in the Bag tab — open it →
Sucker punch / haymaker
The most common real attack — an ambush strike from talking range.
Respond — from the hands-up stance: cover/deflect while off-lining, then burst — continuous strikes until he stops — then disengage.
Train — partner throws from conversational range, telegraphed then surprise timing; drill at elevated heart rate; every rep ends moving to an exit.
The stop signal — hands drop, he turns away, covers up or goes down: stop, scan for a second man, exit. Drill it in the Bag tab.
Krav’s answer is continuous attack, not a single counter: one strike rarely stops a determined attacker, it just hands him the next move. Keep every strike gross-motor — adrenaline strips fine control. From hands-up, lead with straight punches driving forward to the face; already chest-to-chest, switch to elbows and knees. No pause to admire a hit. Keep firing until the stop signal — hands drop, he turns away, covers up or goes down — then stop, scan for a second man, and leave. No chasing. The bursts for each situation are in the Bag tab.
Shove / two-hand grab
The most common precursor to a fight.
Respond — base, clear/break the hands, create distance; default to leave.
Train — grab-break reps, then partner escalates (shove → grab → strike); you choose break-and-leave vs break-and-counter off his follow-up.
Chokes — front & rear
Dangerous and fast. The airway is the priority.
Respond — immediate airway defense; clear the hands while countering to make him release, create space, escape.
Train — mechanics slow first, then progressive resistance on the clear-and-counter; controlled airway-pressure reps with a safety word; rear version from a startle.
Two jobs at once. Clear the grip: your hands drive up-and-out against his thumbs/wrists to peel the hands off your throat, while you tuck your chin down to shield the windpipe. Simultaneously counter (the straight strike, or drive forward into him) so he has to deal with something other than choking you. Sequence: chin down → clear → counter-burst → stop signal → leave. Airway first, always — a choke can put you out in seconds, so nothing else happens until the pressure is off your neck.
Headlock / bear hug
Ambush at grappling range; the risk is being lifted or taken down.
Respond — base low (don’t get lifted), protect neck/airway, create space at the hips/head, escape to standing.
Train — base + space-creation from each variant against a resisting partner; every rep ends standing and moving away.
Wall pin / cornered
Back to a wall, distance gone.
Respond — off-line immediately (head and body off the wall line), open an exit lane, move toward it.
Train — from back-against-wall with a pinning partner; drill converting “cornered” back into distance + exit; reps in tight space.
Pinned, all his force runs in one straight line — from him, through you, into the wall behind you. While your spine is on that line you’re stuck absorbing it. Getting off the line means rotating or slipping so your head and torso are no longer between him and the wall — you pivot to the side so the wall ends up beside you, not behind you. The instant you’re off the line his force spills past into the wall, you’ve got an angle, and there’s an open lane. Cornered → off-line → lane → exit.
Ground / mounted
Where you least want to be — especially with a second attacker.
Respond — protect head, buck/trap/shrimp to make space, GET UP, disengage. Do not stay to grapple.
Train — technical get-ups from mount/guard/pinned; every rep ends standing and leaving, never holding a position.
Mounted = he’s sitting on your torso, the worst spot — especially with a second attacker. Buck and trap: trap one of his arms and the same-side leg, then bridge your hips up hard — it rolls him off to that trapped side. The instant he’s displaced, post up to your feet and leave — don’t stay to grapple. The standing version (technical stand-up): one hand posts behind you, opposite foot plants, lift your hips and slide the trailing leg out to stand, staying faced toward him the whole time. Both share the rule: the rep ends standing and moving away, never holding a position.
Weapon when cornered
Highest-risk category. The trained answer is not to be here.
Respond — distance / give up property / run, first and always. Only if truly cornered: control the weapon limb, create the gap, escape.
Train — “give the wallet, make distance, run” reps first and most; control-and-escape against a training knife only for the cornered case, progressive resistance. Treat any weapon defense as a low-percentage last resort.
Control the limb, not the blade — don’t chase the point. Get two points of contact on the arm: grip the wrist and above the elbow so you own the whole lever. Pin or redirect that arm away from your centerline and keep it moving so he can’t reset or slash back across you. Never take a hand off that arm while the weapon is live. It’s a two-hands-committed, get-to-escape task — not a strike-trade — and it’s the technique that decays fastest without live reps, so drill it most and trust it least. First choice is still distance / give up property / run.
Defending family while engaged
You’re in contact and they need to move.
Respond — interpose, burst until he stops, then extract. Fight only enough to move them.
Train — partner is the threat, another plays family; reps are command-interpose-extract, ending with family at the exit. Never pursue.
Training methodology
What class under-trains
Progressive resistance — a technique only counts once it works against a partner at 70–90%.
Stress inoculation — spike your heart rate first (use your HIIT). Fine motor skills vanish at 150+ bpm; train what survives that.
Decision drilling — partner randomly holds distance / closes / shows a “weapon” / flanks; you call the branch aloud — leave, boundary, act, extract. This is the highest-value rep for your goal.
Escape-to-safety reps — every scenario ends by moving to a set exit. Never end standing over a downed partner — that trains the overcommitment that gets people charged.
Cadence: a 15–20 min decision-drill block ~2×/week, bolted onto a day you’re already warm; one longer scenario session monthly. Technique depth you already get in class — this adds the decisions, the stress, and the escape-first reflex.
After it happens
Stop the instant you can leave. You call 911 first and report you were attacked. Get to witnesses. Say little — “I was attacked, I defended myself, I’ll cooperate” — then a lawyer before any detailed statement. Photograph your injuries; write down the cues that made you fear harm — that is your s.34 evidence.
The one thing
Decide it now, not in the moment. The rep that matters most is making leaving your reflex — pre-load the command word, the rally point, the boundary line. Skill is the backup to the plan, not the plan. And keep training Krav: it is the floor the whole thing stands on.
Preparedness framework, not legal advice — “reasonable” is decided by a court on the facts. A short consult with a BC criminal-defense lawyer on s.34/s.35 for your specific situations (home, with the girls, in public) is the one thing that makes the legal half airtight.

Cus’s Corner

Between rounds, a fighter gets about a minute and a few words from his corner. This page is your corner: a small bank of two- and three-word cues, each aimed at one weak point, rehearsed until they fire on their own, and tested against a number.

The Idea
Cus put his voice in Tyson’s head.
Put yours in your own.
A corner that lives inside someone else can be taken away. A corner you built, rehearsed and tested can’t.
The Case
Fear is fireATTRIBUTED
“The hero and the coward feel the same thing. It’s what they do that’s different.” — Cus D’Amato, as widely quoted
D’Amato taught that fear is like fire: it can heat your house or burn it down. The goal was never to stop feeling it. It was to stop being surprised by it.
A fighter who knows the dressing-room dread arrives every time can treat it as information, not a verdict on whether he belongs in the ring.
The exact wording of both lines varies between sources. The idea is consistent across them.
How he built TysonVERIFIED
Hypnosis. Tyson has said he was hypnotized before sparring, training and fights from about 13 or 14 to the end of his career. The hypnotist was John Halpin, and Cus often sat in and spoke to Tyson directly while he was under.
Autosuggestion. Biographer Mark Kriegel reports Cus distilled Coué’s autosuggestion into a repeated mantra about being the best fighter in the world.
Numbered punches. Combinations were drilled by number until they fired without a decision. That part is a rehearsed cue system, and it is the part this page keeps.
It worked, then it broke. Cus died in 1985, a year before Tyson became the youngest heavyweight champion. The identity had been installed from outside, so it had no way to repair itself once the voice was gone.
Hypnosis is from Tyson’s own interviews (Joe Rogan, Good Trouble). The mantra and the “couldn’t tell where the myth ended” reading are Kriegel’s (Baddest Man), reported second-hand here.
The lesson for this page. Keep what was rehearsed and specific (the numbered punches). Drop what was installed and global (“you’re the best God ever made”). And make sure the voice is yours.
What Holds Up
Short self-talk cues improve performance B — a meta-analysis of 32 studies found a moderate effect (about 0.48). Strongest for fine-motor and newly learned tasks (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011). A 2023 update found the effect still holds, but a decade of research added little understanding.
Instructional beats motivational for skill B — a cue that says what to do (“elbow up”) beats a pep talk on precision tasks. Motivational cues help more with effort and endurance.
Rehearsed beats improvised B — interventions that trained the self-talk worked better than ones that didn’t. That is what the Drill tab is for.
Both sides of the record beats a flattering claim C — people with low self-esteem who repeated “I’m a lovable person” felt worse. Those who weighed when it was true and when it wasn’t came out better (Wood, Perunovic & Lee, 2009). One set of lab studies.
“Excited” beats “calm down” C — saying “I am excited” before singing, a speech or a math test improved performance versus trying to calm down (Brooks, 2014). Lab studies, modest samples.
Hypnosis D — reviews find it associated with better athletic performance, but most studies had no control group. Possibly useful, thinly proven, and it needs a practitioner.
Global affirmations AVOID — “I am the greatest” can backfire for anyone who doesn’t already believe it. The Hard Lines page says the same: not daily chanting.
Three Kinds of Cue
Release
Drops a thought or feeling’s authority. Concedes it exists. “Not relevant.”
Action
Points at the next move. Short and technical. “Next sentence only.”
Effort
For pushing through, not for precision. “Is this 100%?”
The Rules
One trigger per cue. Each cue fires in one named situation. A cue that fires everywhere becomes background noise. “Not relevant” works partly because it fires only on optimization thoughts.
Three in active rotation. Each cue needs its own rehearsal. Mark three as active below; the page won’t take a fourth.
In the moment, not about the moment. Use the cue live. Review how it went on Sunday. Never run a checklist during a conversation: watching yourself perform is what breaks the performance.
Keep it only if the number moves. Test one cue at a time on the Test tab. A cue that changes nothing countable gets retired.
The guardrail. Building cues can turn into busy self-improvement that displaces the harder work. If you’re polishing this page instead of using it, that’s the thought to call not relevant.

How to Test a Cue
Pick one cue and one countable thing it should move: minutes per paper, redirects per block, words written, times you raised your voice.
Run A-B-A-B: a week without the cue, a week with it, a week without, a week with. The page tells you which phase you’re in.
If the number improves in both B weeks, the cue is earning its place. If it doesn’t, retire it and try another. That’s a finding, not a failure.
This is the same reversal design as Self-Evidence’s “one variable, then reverse it.” Four weeks of one person’s data is a reading, not proof.

Work Efficiency

Throughput is not the constraint. Selection is. What gets picked, what comes off the plate entirely, how few things stay open — and one number watched over weeks. Experiment 1 of an ongoing set.

The trap
Faster at the wrong workB
There are two ways to be busy. One moves the thing that matters. The other clears a list. Both produce the same feeling at four in the afternoon, which is why the difference is almost impossible to see from inside the day.
The tell is at the end of the week, not the end of the day. A week where every day felt productive and the one thing that mattered did not move is the failure this page exists for. It is not a motivation problem and it is not a speed problem. It is a selection problem, and speed makes it worse.
So efficiency is the wrong first lever for you. Tooling, batching and faster hands applied to a badly selected week give you more of the week you already had. Every ordinary productivity system is built for someone who cannot get started. Running one of those is pouring fuel into the failure mode.
Scope — what this page is not. The Honest Stop owns where to stop on a task you have already picked: done or covered, the line, the cut. Entropy owns upkeep and drift. Organic Design owns stopping being the node. Shaping and Adapting owns the relevance gate and the outside judge. The three-tier rule on the brief owns tempo. None of them say which work. That is this page: what gets picked, what gets handed off, and how few things are open at once.
The method — how every experiment in this set runs
One variable, four weeks, one numberA
Name the single change. One. Two changes at once and you learn nothing from either, because you cannot attribute the result. This is the rule that makes the set worth keeping rather than a pile of good intentions.
Name the number that would move if it worked — before you start, in writing, on the Log tab. If you cannot name it, you do not yet know what you are testing.
Run four weeks, then decide: keep, kill, or adjust. Four is long enough to survive one bad week and short enough that a dud dies cheap. The decision is made from the numbers, not from how it felt.
Recording the number is not admin — it is most of the effect. Monitoring progress toward a goal measurably improves attainment, and it works better when the progress is physically written down. The log is not bookkeeping about the intervention. It is part of the intervention.
The six moves
1Count the week before you change itA
One week. Change nothing. Just count. A baseline you did not measure is a story you will tell yourself later.
Use when starting any experiment in this set, including this one. Week one is always observation.
Four numbers, once a week — minutes you spent on in-class logistics, minutes on marking, whether the primary moved, and how many things are open and half-done on Sunday night.
Estimate badly rather than not at all — a rough tally beaten out on Sunday is worth more than an exact one you abandon by Thursday. Precision is not the point; direction over weeks is.
Do not improve anything in week one — the temptation is enormous and it destroys the comparison. Write the numbers down and leave them alone.
Room 148 Logistics means handing out, taking in, settling, repeating instructions, hunting for the dongle — anything that is not teaching or assessing.
Marking Count the real total across the week, including the parts done at home after everyone is in bed.
2One primary, and a kill listA
Name the one thing that would make the week worth it — and name what you are not doing to pay for it.
Use when the week is being set, Sunday or Monday morning, before anything is in motion.
One primary for the week, written as a result — not a topic, not an area. If only this moved, the week was worth having.
Three things on the kill list, in writing — named projects you are explicitly not doing this term. Not deprioritised. Not later. Not doing.
The kill list is the half that works — a primary without one is a wish, because everything else stays live and keeps bidding for the same hours.
A new idea does not displace the primary mid-week — it goes to Sunday. That is already the rule on the brief; this is where the rule earns its keep.
Income Primary: one module is ready for a pilot user. Not: the book, the second book, a new page on the OS.
Room 148 Primary: the next unit's worksheets exist. Not: rebuilding a generator that already works.
Kill list Written on paper, read on Sunday, and genuinely untouched until the term ends.
Specific, named goals beat “do your best” reliably. The kill list is the part with no study behind it — it is here because the documented failure is low-impact work crowding out high-impact work, and a primary alone does not stop that.
3Estimate, then logB
Two numbers per task: what you thought it would take, what it took. Build your own multiplier instead of trusting your sense of time.
Use when you start any task you will do again — a lesson build, a set of marking, a page, a report-card batch.
Write the estimate before you start — in minutes, on the Log tab, one line. Guess freely; a bad guess recorded is data, a good guess unrecorded is nothing.
Write the actual when you stop — honestly, including the part where you wandered off.
After about fifteen tasks you have a personal multiplier — the typical ratio of actual to estimate. Use it. If your multiplier is 1.8, two hours of planned work is a three-and-a-half-hour evening, and the planning that assumed two hours was fiction.
The multiplier is the whole deliverable here — not faster work. A true number about your own time changes what you agree to.
Marking “22 ELL paragraphs” — estimate 45, actual 80. Three of those and the week has no evening left in it.
Forge OS “One protocol page” — the estimate here has been wrong in the same direction for months.
The planning fallacy — systematic underestimation of your own task durations — is well established, and the remedy that holds up is using your own past record of similar tasks rather than reasoning afresh about this one.
4Hand the room its own operationsBC
Every minute of in-class logistics you do yourself is a minute bought at full price. Most of it is work a fifteen-year-old can own, and owning it is worth more to them than watching you do it.
Use when the logistics number from move 1 is the biggest of the four — which it probably is.
The test for a real job: if nobody does it, does something break? If nothing breaks, it is decoration, and a teenager will know inside a week. Every job must come off your plate, not be invented for them.
Rotate, don't appoint — a fixed chief of staff is more efficient and teaches nobody anything about themselves. Two weeks per job, assigned not volunteered, or the keenest student takes everything and the quiet ones learn they are not the sort who gets picked.
Start with two jobs, not a system — the timekeeper and the log. Three weeks. If the logistics number drops and both jobs run without reminding, add three more.
One line per rotation is the whole reflection — “what did this job need from you that came easily, and what did it need that didn't?” On a card you keep. Nine cards at term's end is their Personal and Social self-assessment, already written.
Hard line: no job touches another student's marks, attendance or personal information. Not even handing back a pile. Give materials the unmarked work only.
ELL 1/2 Timekeeper and reset need almost no English — they are the two to give first to a student eleven months in the country.
ELL 1/2 Onboarding: a named student owns a new arrival's first three days. Your largest recurring time sink, handed to the person best placed to do it.
Room 148 The log: what was done, what is owed. Kills “what did I miss” permanently.
ENG8 Speaking is assessed here, so the verbal jobs — MC, discussion chair — are curriculum, not housekeeping.
FLA 6/7 Don't. Rotating classroom jobs with adults reads as patronising. There it is asking one person, once, as a favour.
Responsibility and competence effects are solid ground. The claim that this teaches a fifteen-year-old what career suits them is not — what it reliably gives them is language for describing their own work, which is the thing almost no nineteen-year-old can do in an interview.
5Batch it, and put a ceiling on markingB
Same kind of work, one block. And a fixed minutes-per-piece decided before you open the pile, because marking expands to fill whatever is left of your life.
Use when the same type of task is scattered across the week, and every single time you mark.
One kind of work per block — all the marking, then all the building. Switching between kinds carries a real cost each time, and eleven fragments of marking cost more than one block of the same total length.
Set the per-piece ceiling in advance — four minutes, six, whatever you choose, times the number of pieces, and that is the budget. A timer, visible. When the ceiling hits, the piece is finished.
Cap the comments, don't cap the care — a small number of targeted, actionable comments does more than comprehensive correction, and comprehensive correction is where your evenings go. One thing to fix next, named, is the standard.
Mark against the Check part — the tick-box outcomes are already on the sheet in student language. Marking what is already named is faster and more consistent than marking freehand.
Marking 22 paragraphs at four minutes is 88 minutes, set and visible, not “an evening”.
Room 148 Build all four worksheets in one sitting, not one per night across the week.
Switch costs are real and measured, but the figures that circulate about them — losing 40 per cent of your productivity, needing 23 minutes to refocus — are far stronger than the evidence supports. The direction holds; the numbers are folklore. On written feedback specifically: that focused beats comprehensive is the better-supported position, but the second-language correction literature is genuinely contested and you know it better than I do — treat the ceiling as a time instrument, not a pedagogical claim.
6Close before you openC
Count the things that are open and half-done. Keep the count low. An unfinished thing charges rent on attention whether or not you are working on it.
Use when starting anything new, and on Sunday when you count.
A hard cap on open items — pick a number, three or four, and finish one before starting another. The cap is arbitrary on purpose so it cannot be argued with at ten at night.
Finishing beats starting, even on something worse — a closed mediocre thing costs nothing further. An open excellent thing costs every day it stays open.
Park, don't juggle — the park list already exists on the planner. A parked thing is closed for the purposes of the count; a thing you are “coming back to this week” is not.
Forge OS Pages built and not deployed have been the standing example. Deployed or parked; nothing in between.
Income One module finished and sent beats three modules at eighty per cent.
Three tools
The week cardSunday · 3 min
Four numbers on the Log tab: logistics minutes, marking minutes, did the primary move, open items. Plus the primary, named. That is the experiment's whole instrument panel.
The estimate logPer task · 15 sec
Task, estimate, actual. The Log tab computes the multiplier for you once there are a few entries. Fifteen entries is where it becomes trustworthy.
The rotation cardFortnightly · 1 line
An index card per student, one line per rotation: what came easily, what didn't. You keep them. They get them back at the end of term and write one paragraph about the pattern.
Start Monday
1Open the Log tab and write this week's primary before anything else happens. One result.
2Write three things on the kill list. On paper, somewhere you will see it.
3Change nothing else this week. Count only: logistics, marking, primary, open items.
4Log an estimate and an actual for every task you do twice or more.
5Sunday: fill the week card. Compare nothing yet — one week is a dot, not a line.
6Week two: name the two jobs, give them away, keep counting. That is the first real change, and it is the only one.
7End of week four: keep, kill, or adjust, from the numbers.
Sunday block — three questions
Did the primary move, or did a full week of other work happen instead?
What did I do myself this week that someone in the room could have owned?
How far off was my estimate, and in which direction?
These are not wired into the debrief. Three questions, read off this page on Sunday, until there is reason to build them in.
The one thing
What would make this week worth it — and what am I not doing to pay for it?
Asked Monday morning, in writing. The second half is the half that works.
Failure modes
Optimising the week you should have cut — the whole trap, restated. Faster at work that should not exist is the failure this page is for, and it is the one most likely to come back.
The log becomes the project — charts, fields, a better tracker. Building the measuring instrument is itself low-impact work that feels like progress. Four numbers, three minutes, Sunday. If it grows, it has become the thing it was meant to catch.
Two changes at once — then nothing is learned and the whole set is just activity with better vocabulary.
A kill list that is really a later list — if you expect to do it in March, it is not on the kill list and it is still spending your attention now.
Jobs invented to be jobs — decoration. The students detect it faster than any adult audience and the system dies quietly.
The ceiling used as an excuse — it is a budget for a known pile, not permission to stop caring. The standard is still one thing to fix next, named.
Reading this instead of counting — week one is four numbers. Nothing on this page does anything until those exist.
The queue — experiments 2 to 6
2. The teaching load audit. Where the week actually goes, block by block, for two weeks — then one structural change to the worst block.
3. Build once, use many. How much of what you make each week is reusable next year, and what would make it reusable by default.
4. The two hours. Two protected hours a week is the real building capacity in term time. The experiment is whether a fixed slot beats a floating one.
5. The summer block. Capacity is the break, so the experiment runs backwards: what has to be decided in May for July to be worth anything.
6. Delegation past the classroom. What comes off the plate that is not student work — tooling, templates, and what is genuinely someone else's job.
Order is not fixed. Each one gets its own four weeks and its own number, and none of them start while this one is running.
Evidence
A Strong. Monitoring progress toward a goal improves attainment, with larger effects when progress is physically recorded (Harkin et al. 2016, meta-analysis) — this is the case for the log being part of the intervention rather than admin around it. Specific, named goals outperform “do your best” (Locke & Latham 2002) — the case for one named primary written as a result.

B Good, with variance. The planning fallacy — systematic underestimation of one's own task durations — is robust (Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994), and the remedy with the best support is taking the outside view from your own record of similar past tasks (Kahneman & Lovallo 1993). Task-switching carries a measurable time cost (Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans 2001). Autonomy and competence as drivers of engagement (Deci & Ryan, self-determination theory) — the case for real jobs rather than decorative ones. Feedback works better when it is specific and actionable than when it is exhaustive (Shute 2008 review).

C Extrapolated. The open-item cap, and the claim that an unfinished task charges ongoing attention, lean on the Zeigarnik line of work, which has replicated unevenly. The reasoning is sound and the specific cap is not tested. Likewise the claim that rotating jobs teaches self-knowledge: the exposure and the vocabulary are plausible, the self-insight is not demonstrated.

D Arbitrary. Four weeks per experiment, three items on the kill list, two jobs to start, the open-item cap, the minutes-per-piece ceiling, fifteen entries before trusting the multiplier. No evidence sets any of these. They are fixed in advance precisely so they cannot be renegotiated in the moment.

X Taught but not supported. “Multitasking costs 40 per cent of your productivity” and “it takes 23 minutes to refocus” — both far exceed what the underlying studies show. Inbox zero as a productivity intervention. The 25-minute Pomodoro as anything other than an arbitrary number that works because it is fixed. “Touch it once.” Any system whose first move is to get you started faster — wrong failure mode for you, and it accelerates the one you have.

One honest caveat on move 5. That focused feedback beats comprehensive correction is the better-supported position, but written corrective feedback in second-language writing is an actively contested literature with twenty-five years of argument in it. Treat the ceiling as a budget for your time, not as a settled claim about ELL pedagogy.

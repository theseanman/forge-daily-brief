The Honest Stop

Done, or covered? Set the line by what the result needs. Stop at it on the things that don’t matter. Go one step past it on the things that do.

The trap
Two kinds of effortB
Effort aimed at the outcome asks what would make this actually work for the person it’s for. Effort aimed at the alibi asks what would let you say you did everything, if it goes wrong. From the outside they often look the same, which is why they are hard to tell apart from the inside.
The tell is where the effort stops. Outcome effort stops when the job does what it is for. Alibi effort stops when the uneasy feeling goes quiet — when you feel covered. Those two stopping points are often in different places.
The mismatch runs both ways. Sometimes the feeling quiets before the job is done, so you stop short while feeling you tried. Sometimes it keeps nagging after the job is done, so you pile on effort that helps no one. Covered can look like less effort or like more.
Not more effort. Better-aimed effort. “Beyond good enough” applied to everything turns into polishing, and polishing is where your time already leaks. This protocol goes past the line in a few chosen places, on purpose, and stops sooner everywhere else. The step past the line is paid for by the cut.
The five moves, in order
1Set the line firstA
Decide what finished looks like before you start, in terms of the result — not your effort.
Use when you begin anything that matters: a task, a conversation you have been putting off, a piece of help someone needs.
Write it as a result for them — “she can do tonight’s questions on her own,” not “I spend an hour on her homework.”
Make it checkable — someone else could walk in and say whether it has been met. This is your done-when, aimed outward.
If you can’t write the line, stop there — you don’t yet know what the effort is for, and effort with no line always stops at covered.
Home Not “talk to her about it,” but “she knows she can come to me about it again.”
Room 148 Not “mark the essays,” but “each student knows the one thing to fix next.”
Income Not “work on the course,” but “module two is ready for a pilot user to try.”
2The stop checkAC
At the moment you stop, or notice you can’t, ask one question: done, or covered?
Use when you are about to put something down — or you have finished and cannot seem to put it down.
Done — the line is met. Stop. Any unease left over is noise, not information.
Covered — you feel you have done enough, but the line is not met. Take one concrete move toward it, not a pile of effort to quiet the feeling.
Still going past done — that is polishing. Stop, and spend the time somewhere on your past-the-line list.
Home You explained the fractions once, she nodded, you are heading back to the laptop. Covered. One move: watch her do one question alone.
Room 148 The deck meets the plan. You are now adjusting a shade of navy. Polishing. Close it.
The if-then form is the tested part. The question itself is this protocol’s reasoning, not a studied intervention.
3One step past the lineD
In a few chosen places, once the line is met, take one more step that nobody would check or notice was missing.
Use when the line is met on something on your past-the-line list: the girls first, then the one primary project. Nowhere else.
One step, named — decide what it is before you take it. One, not a second project.
The test is invisibility — if anyone would notice it missing, it was part of the line. The step past it is the one only you would know about.
It closes tonight — a step past the line never opens a loop that survives the night. If it would, it is a new project and it waits for Sunday.
Home Dinner done, everyone fed, nothing left on any list. You ask about the thing she mentioned yesterday, the one she assumed you had forgotten.
Home Homework finished. You stay two minutes longer and ask which part felt easy.
Income The module is ready. You send it to one real person and ask a single question about it.
This is why an alibi can never be the motive: an alibi only needs what others can see. A step no one will see is done for them.
4The cutB
On everything else, stop at the line or before it. Covering yourself on the unimportant is paid for out of the important.
Use when the task is not on your past-the-line list — which is most tasks.
Stop at the line — met is finished. No second pass for peace of mind.
Name the list once — decide which few things get the step past the line, and write them down, so the cut is never renegotiated in the moment.
Count what the cut buys — the minutes saved here are the ones that pay for move 3.
Fantasy Lineup set. Three more waiver articles will not change it.
Email The reply is clear and correct. Send it; no second draft “just in case.”
Forge OS It works and it is readable. The next refinement waits until something is actually wrong.
5The weekly look backD
Once a week, two questions, asked in the Sunday review you already run. Nothing new to track.
Where did I stop at covered instead of done?
Where did I polish past done on something that didn’t matter?
The first shows where the line was missed. The second shows where the time for move 3 went instead.
The one thing
Done, or covered?
Ask it at the moment you stop. Stop when the result is there, not when the feeling goes quiet — and where it counts most, take one step past the line that only you would know about.
Failure modes
Running it live — the question belongs at stopping points. Asked in the middle of an hour with the girls, it becomes the auditor with a clipboard, and attention on your own performance takes attention off them.
Past the line everywhere — the step spreads to every task and becomes polishing with a better name. Keep the list short.
The step as a project — “one step past the line” turns into a new build. If it opens a loop that survives the night, it is not a step.
The verdict spiral — “covered” is a reading, not a judgement of you. One honest answer, one move, then drop it.
Treating real consequences as alibi — going to work because the mortgage needs it, or driving the limit because tickets are real, is outcome effort. The question is about where you stop, not why you started.
This page as the alibi — reading about the honest stop feels like making one. The page is worth something only if it changes where you stop this week.
Evidence
A Strong. Implementation intentions — the “when X, I will Y” form of the stop check roughly doubles follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis). Specific goals outperform vague “do your best” goals (Locke & Latham 2002), which is the case for writing the line first.

B Good, with variance. People adjust effort to protect how they will see themselves if things go badly: self-handicapping (Berglas & Jones 1978) and self-worth theory (Covington 1992). A close cousin of the covered pattern, not an exact match. People who try to optimize every choice report lower well-being than those who stop at good enough (Schwartz et al. 2002; correlational) — the case for the cut. Regrets of inaction last longer than regrets of action (Gilovich & Medvec 1995). Attention to the steps of a practised skill disrupts it (Beilock & Carr 2001) — the case against running this live.

C Extrapolated. The done-or-covered distinction — that the stopping point reveals the motive — is reasoning built on the work above. It fits the evidence and has not been tested as such.

D Arbitrary. One step past the line, the short list, the invisibility test and the two weekly questions. No evidence sets these. They are fixed in advance so they can’t be renegotiated in the moment.

Deliberately excluded: “always give 110%” and “exceed expectations in everything” — they feed the polishing leak. Also excluded: the test “would you still do this if no one judged you?” It confuses real consequences with judgement and misfires on ordinary obligations.

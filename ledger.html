The Grievance Protocol (I don’t deserve this)

What to do with the belief that good conduct earns a return. The ledger is an implicit contract — treat people well, be respectful, do the work, and receive proportionate treatment back. The other party never signed it.

The Premise
Nothing is owed.
Waiting to be paid is weakness.
The ledger is not held as an argument, which is why arguing with it does nothing. It is held as an expectation, running underneath, and it only shows itself when it is violated. Knowing the world is not fair is a proposition. The expectation never heard it.
The Pillars
01The Contract
A receivable nobody signed for.
The pain is not the slight. It is the breach of a contract the other party was never party to. That is why the same feeling shows up across situations that have nothing else in common.
It is protective, not stupid. “I don’t deserve this” is a defence of your own worth when something has made you feel small. Seeing its function loosens it; calling it irrational does not.
It never closes. The claim is filed with a judge who is not there, so the case reopens every time. The loop is the cost, not the slight.
Grade: Weak-to-moderate. Psychological entitlement predicts more anger and perceived injustice after impersonal bad luck (Zitek & Jordan, Personality and Individual Differences). Honest caveat from the same work: when people recalled being treated unfairly by a specific person, entitlement did not change how angry they got. So this bites hardest where there is nobody to blame — traffic, circumstance, the way things fell.
02Tier One · Nothing Survives The Night
Traffic. The slight in the hallway. The tone in the email.
Fire the handle: Nothing is owed. One beat. Do not argue with the ledger — deny its authority.
Put attention on one sense channel immediately. Hands on the wheel, the sound of the room. Two seconds, not a procedure.
Scope it. It fires on the ledger only. It is not a general silencer — some internal signal is load-bearing.
This is the same mechanic as “not relevant”, which already works for you: it concedes the thought exists and denies it authority, rather than trying to stop it existing. Same grammar, different target. If a phrase stops landing after a few weeks, that is data about the phrase, not about you — change it.
03Tier Two · Consequential, In Front Of You
The last-minute request that lands after the work is already done.
Drop the demand, keep the preference. “I’d rather this hadn’t landed” is true and passes. “This shouldn’t have landed, I don’t deserve it” is the loop. You lose nothing by the swap — you still want the thing, you have only dropped the claim that you were owed it.
Then the only question that pays: what does this actually change about what I do next?
Answer the request on its merits, not on the balance of the account. The account is not evidence about whether to say yes.
Grade: Moderate. The demand-to-preference move is Ellis’s. A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis of fifty years of REBT found medium effects both on outcomes and on irrational beliefs themselves (David et al., Journal of Clinical Psychology; 68 studies in the between-group analysis). The therapy package is better evidenced than the specific demandingness mechanism isolated on its own — graded on the package, applied as the mechanism.
04Tier Three · It Actually Matters
Do not reach for the handle here.
Let it land. The hurt is the correct response to a loss, not a malfunction to be managed down.
Name it precisely. Not “I’m upset” but the specific thing — what happened, in front of whom, and what it threatened. Precision is what does the work; vague naming does not.
Set the desert question aside explicitly. “Did I deserve this” cannot be settled from inside your own head and does not converge. “What do I do tomorrow” can be answered.
For the distancing move — your own name, second person, zooming out in time — use The Resilience Protocol, Pillar 04. It is already built and graded there. Do not run a second version of it here.
The reason the tiers exist: using the Tier One handle on something that genuinely hurts is suppression, not strength, and suppression is reliably worse than reappraisal on most outcomes. A single universal move either trivialises the big cases or over-processes the small ones.
05The Scope
Where the premise stops.
The premise is about strangers, work, and the world. It is not about your children.
Obligations between a father and his children are real. A version of this that teaches you to expect nothing there would cost you something you actually want.
The tell: if the ledger is running on someone in your own house, the problem is the account, not the person. They cannot see the accounting from outside, but they can feel it.
The second guard: a premise this hard, pointed at a person rather than at the world, turns into contempt without much help. Contempt reliably closes doors you are trying to keep open.
The Exemplars
Miyamoto Musashi
Dokkōdō, 1645 · written days before his death
Tier 1 · his words, in translation
Resentment and complaint are appropriate neither for oneself nor others.
Precept nine of twenty-one. Translator not established — the widely circulated English text is unattributed, so treat the wording as a translation rather than as his exact phrasing.
Tier 2 · the gloss (Clyde)
He does not argue that the complaint is incorrect. He rules it out as a move. That is the whole difference between understanding the ledger and being free of it.
Most of the twenty-one are renunciations of expected return — including one that says to respect the gods without counting on their help. He kept the conduct and dropped the account.
John Boyd
The fork in the road · recorded in Robert Coram’s biography
Tier 1 · his spoken words, as recorded
To be somebody or to do something.
Said to the young officers he mentored. Recollected speech from Coram’s Boyd: The Fighter Pilot Who Changed the Art of War, not a line from one of his briefings — tagged so the two are never confused.
Tier 2 · the gloss (Clyde)
The fork is the ledger. To be somebody is to collect the return. To do something is to do the work and renounce the account.
He took the second and it cost him assignments, promotions and the favour of his superiors. He knew the price before he chose. That is what makes it a renunciation rather than a consolation.

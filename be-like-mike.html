Be Like Mike

The peekaboo as D’Amato built it and Tyson fought it, 1985–88, rebuilt for a solo trainer with a BOB, a Quest, medicine balls and kettlebells. Four layers: technique, power, conditioning, the fight mind. The Size and Strength plan is not touched; Cus’s Corner keeps the cue bank and the D’Amato case study.

Change
Two constraints run everything. The ankle rules on the Size and Strength page bar jumping, sprinting, lunges and step-ups for the twelve weeks unless your physio clears them — so no Tyson bounce yet: footwork is step-and-pivot, power work is planted. And you are 47 and 210 lbs with a day job that needs your hands. The famous Tyson numbers are not the target. The principles are.

Why it is an OODA weapon
It attacks Observe and Orient, not Decide and Act
Most styles try to be faster or hit harder. The peekaboo makes the other man’s picture stale. D
The head never stops. He observes a target, orients a punch at it, and by Act the target has moved.
Slip and counter are one motion. There is no gap between his miss and your hit for him to re-orient in.
Closing speed removes the distance in which his loop can run at all. Tempo, not raw speed — Boyd’s point exactly.
The numbers were private. Rooney’s calls were real information to Tyson and noise to the opponent. Deception at Observe. B
What Tyson actually trained
Reported, not verified
The repeated prime-Tyson day: up at 4 a.m. for a three-mile run, back to bed, oatmeal, technique and ten rounds of sparring around noon, calisthenics in enormous volume (2,000 sit-ups, 500–800 dips and push-ups, 500 shrugs, ten minutes of neck bridges), then bag, slip bag and mitts. D
Rooney confirms the 4 a.m. run and the oatmeal in at least one account. The calisthenic totals are confirmed by nobody who was there and are quoted with suspicious uniformity — usually the sign of one source copied many times. D’Amato distrusted weights; Tyson’s lifting came later and was limited.
Take four principles, not the numbers: head movement drilled daily for rounds; the neck as a weapon system; conditioning in rounds because the fight is; and the whole thing structured by someone else.
Layer one · Technique
STANCE
The frame
Feet shoulder width, slightly squared, knees bent, weight on the balls of the feet. Hands high at the cheeks, palms in, elbows tight, chin down. The guard is a frame to parry and launch from; the defence is the head and the legs. Your orthodox conventional style will make the squared stance feel wrong for two weeks. D
The old flaw costs more here. The squared stance exposes the right jaw. For two weeks every shadow jab finishes with the right hand consciously touched to the right cheek. The slip bag punishes the drift after that.
RUNG 1
The slip line
A rope at chin height across a hallway.
Walk it slipping alternately either side of the line, knees and waist doing the work, head moving no more than a fist’s width from centre. Then duck under and up the other side, slip, duck, over. Ten minutes a day. D
The measure is boredom. When ten minutes feels like nothing, move up.
RUNG 2
The slip bag
A tennis ball in a sock from a doorframe, at head height.
Set it swinging, slip, let it return, slip. The head slips; the feet stay under you. Every slip ends with the hands back at the cheeks — this is where the right-hand fix lives. D
Gate: 20 clean slips each side → add the counter: slip and shadow-punch past the ball in the same motion. The 2 off an outside slip left, the 1 off an outside slip right.
Gate: 20 clean slip-and-counters → slip with a six-inch step in, so every slip closes distance. Defence and entry become one event.
RUNG 3
Numbers in shadow
Three-minute rounds, numbered combinations off a slip or a duck: 1-2-1, 2-3-2, 6-3 off a duck, 7-2-5, 5-2 off a slip. Pairs and threes from a low crouch, head moved after the last punch every time. D
Until you have a caller, write five random three-number strings on a card before each round and read the next as you reset.
Gate: any three-number string thrown without a pause to translate → take the numbers onto BOB.
RUNG 4
The wall
A foot from a wall, facing it. Bob, weave and slip for a minute without touching it, then throw combinations without stepping back. D
This is what made Tyson frightening: staying in the pocket after arriving. Most fighters earn the inside and give it back.
RUNG 5
BOB
Rounds: slip an imagined jab, step in, 1-2 or 5-2 to the taped numbers, move the head, pivot off at an angle, repeat. Bare-handed on vinyl is acceptable at 60–70% power, straight wrist, first two knuckles; three rounds is the ceiling per session until skin and small joints adapt. Hooks and uppercuts to the head zone bare-handed are the wrist-breakers — touch power only. D
Krav bursting does not transfer. The peekaboo is three punches and a reposition, not a stream.
The one purchase worth making: wraps and 14 oz gloves, roughly $60–90 CAD. Not required; it lets Rung 5 run at real power, and real power is the only honest test of the mechanics.
RUNG 6
The Quest
Good for one thing: a moving target that punches back, which nothing else in the house does. Use it for reading and slipping incoming punches and the timing of the counter. It teaches nothing about distance, weight transfer or the pocket. The reaction drill, not the boxing. D
Footwork under the ankle rules. No bounce, no hops. Step-and-pivot only: a short step in behind the slip, a pivot off the lead foot to take an angle after the combination, a step back out. Slower than Tyson’s base and correct until the ankle is cleared. His best angles came off the pivot anyway, so you lose less than it seems.
Layer two · Power
Where power comes from
Filimonov (1985): in masters of sport the rear-leg drive gave about 38% of punching force and trunk rotation about 37%, arm extension only 24%. In novices the arm did nearly 38% and the legs under 17%. The difference between a novice’s punch and an expert’s is almost entirely below the shoulders. C
Loturco et al. (2016): correlations of 0.67–0.85 between punching impact and lower-body power in Brazilian national-team boxers; the jump squat shared ~82% of its variance with impact. Loturco (2021): a short lower-body power block transferred to an ~8% gain in punch impact, transfer coefficient ~0.8. Bench press power did not move impact. B
Your lifting plan already builds most of it — leg press, RDLs, hip thrusts, heavy pressing. What it does not build is rotation and rate: hips turning fast against the ground, passed through the trunk to the fist. That is the gap. Eight minutes, twice a week, planted.
P1
Rotational medicine-ball throw
Side-on to a wall two metres away, ball at the hip, feet planted. Drive the rear hip through, turn, release at chest height. The hip starts it, the arms finish it — same cue as the right hand. 3 × 5 each side, a light ball you can throw hard, full rest. No wall that will take it? Throw to a lawn and walk. B for the class, D for the dose.
P2
Overhead slam
Ball overhead, slam straight down with the trunk, no jump. 3 × 5. The vertical trunk snap of the uppercut, feet planted as the ankle rules require. D
P3
Kettlebell swing
Two-hand, hard hinge, the bell snapping to chest height off the hips, not the arms. 3 × 10. The closest planted movement to the rear-leg drive the research keeps finding. Start a size lighter than you think — the point is speed. D
P4
Neck — isometric and anticipatory
Soccer studies: stronger necks associate with lower head acceleration on impact, but the 2025 systematic review found traditional neck strengthening alone does not reduce it — adding neuromuscular bracing does. C So: not Tyson’s bridges, which at 47 are a cervical-disc gamble you do not need.
Hand on forehead, push and resist 20 s; back of head; each side. Two rounds. Then one slip-bag round where you tense the neck and tuck the chin a half-second before each slip. Three times a week. Shrugs are already in your lift days. D
Where it goes. P1–P3 as an eight-minute block after the Monday and Friday lifts — the two days not squeezed before your evening class. I have assumed the balls and bells are at home; if they live at the school gym, the block stays at the end of the session there. P4 attaches to the home technique block Mon/Wed/Fri. Nothing here goes before the lift: power work is done fresh or not done.
Layer three · Conditioning
Wednesday becomes rounds
A round is three minutes of mixed high-intensity work with a minute off: aerobic across the fight, anaerobic bursts inside each round. High-intensity intervals raise VO2max more than steady work — about as settled as exercise science gets. A
Replace the bike intervals with BOB and shadow rounds, alternating: a shadow round of head movement and numbers, a BOB round of slip-step-combination at 60–70%. 3 min on, 1 off.
Start at four rounds. Add one a week to eight, then cut the rest to 45 s. The last 30 s of each round should feel like the opening of a fight, not the end of a jog. D for the progression.
Sunday stays Zone 2. That base is what you recover on between rounds. A
Exhale sharply on every punch and every slip. It braces, it times, and it stops the held-breath-then-gasp every returning boxer does.
Recovery rule. Sauna Tue/Thu stays. No cold water after the Mon/Fri power blocks — already your rule. New: if Wednesday’s rounds leave you unable to hit Thursday’s lift at normal loads, the rounds were too many, not the lift too heavy. The lifting plan is primary for twelve weeks; this fits around it.
Layer four · The fight mind
Bad intentions
Tyson’s phrase for how he threw. Every punch in every drill, including the ones at touch power, is thrown as if meant to end something: the intent lives in the hip turn and the exhale, not in how hard the hand lands. Shadow boxing without intent builds a shadow boxer. D
Rehearsal
Mental practice improves motor performance when combined with physical practice; strongest for tasks with a cognitive component, which reading and slipping punches is. B
Sixty seconds before each session, eyes closed in the stance: see a jab coming, slip it, feel the 2 land, move the head. Then do it for real.
The pocket as a decision
Staying inside after arriving means accepting you will be hit while you work. Tyson accepted that; most men do not. The decision is made before the round, on the way to BOB, every time. D
The structure lesson
Under D’Amato and Rooney: 35–0, 31 by knockout. D’Amato died in 1985; Rooney was fired in 1988; the slide was visible within two years and the style decayed first — less head movement, more standing and loading up. B for the record; the causal reading is widely shared and partly mine.
The peekaboo only works at full commitment, and Tyson’s was held in place by structure other people provided. You train solo, so the structure has to be built in: the log, the gates, the fixed slots, and one person you show the log to. Boyd says the same: orientation decays without feedback from outside.

Failure modes
Chasing the Tyson numbers. 2,000 sit-ups was never the protocol; head movement for rounds is.
Bouncing because it looks right, on an ankle the rules have not cleared.
Bare-handed BOB at full power in week one; three weeks lost to a wrist.
Wednesday’s rounds eating Thursday’s lift.
Shadow boxing as cardio, head still, hands loose — training the opposite of the style.
Training the style without the structure, so it fades the way it faded for the man himself.
Honest limits
Everything in the technique layer is coaching tradition graded D; nobody has trialled slip bags and nobody will. The power research is real but from small elite samples of young men who already punch well. The neck evidence is from soccer and mixed. And none of this tests the thing that matters most: whether the head movement survives a real punch. That needs a partner, and a partner is out of scope for now. What you can build solo is the rhythm, the slip-as-entry reflex, the planted power and the rounds. Most of the engine. Not the car.
The one thing
Make the slip and the counter one movement. Everything else here exists to make that one movement available, powered and repeatable for eight rounds.

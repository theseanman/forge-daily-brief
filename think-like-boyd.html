Think Like Boyd

The general method, not the episodes. See what is actually happening, pick what matters, observe cheaply, predict people, decide in calm, and think one move past the obvious response. Experiment 2 of the set. Seven skills, one at a time, a fortnight each.

What he is actually doing
Not prophecy. Two things that look like it.
He models people, not events. When Boyd says a man will refuse twice and then agree, he is not seeing the future. He has read what the man is defending, and identity objections are predictable because they are about self-image rather than the proposal, and they run out because there are only so many ways to say the same thing about yourself. Saying the prediction aloud collapses it: a refusal that has been named is a performance both of you can see.
He changes the question instead of answering it. He never tries to win the question on the table. He notices that the decision will be made later by tired people answering whatever question is in front of them, and he goes to work on the question. You cannot win "what did he say in the gym." Nobody has to ask "who tells a hundred families Friday night is cancelled." That is not long-range planning. It is choosing the ground.
Everything else is a notebook. He measures, he writes the observation separately from what it means, he writes the prediction before the event, and he records being wrong with the same weight as being right. The whole apparatus is one notebook, carried everywhere and reread. If this page ever becomes more than that, it has failed.
The loop — ten moves
01Terrain before actionB
Delay the first move until the map exists.
In any new room, say nothing useful for longer than feels comfortable. Who decides, who is believed, what the unwritten rules are, what each procedure costs and whom, what the timetable is. Boyd's own doctrine puts orientation at the centre of the loop, ahead of decision and action, because a fast decision on a bad map is just a fast way to be wrong.
The map has four columns. Who holds power (decides, marks, files). Who holds authority (the room believes them). What the rules are that nobody wrote down. What things cost. The dangerous configuration is power and authority in different people; it never stays stable, and the day it breaks is predictable.
02Measure what everyone assumesC
The gap between the assumed number and the measured one is where the cheap wins live.
Every institution runs on numbers nobody has checked. The bus takes forty minutes. Marking a set takes an evening. That kid is lazy. The meeting is necessary. Pick one a fortnight and measure it; the result is usually wrong by enough to matter and the measurement usually costs less than an hour.
Measured beats clever. A page of real bus times earned more goodwill in a week than anything impressive could have in a term, because it cannot be argued with and costs the recipient nothing to accept. The move does not survive being faked.
03Observation separated from meaningA
Two lines, never one. What you saw. What you think it means.
The interpretation contaminates the observation the moment they share a sentence. "She was dismissive" is a conclusion. "She looked at her laptop while he spoke, twice" is an observation, and it can turn out to mean something else next week. The Watch tab enforces the split with two fields because willpower will not.
Why it matters: once you hold a hypothesis, search becomes confirmatory. You gather evidence for the reading rather than against it, and the case feels strongest exactly when it was built after you picked the answer. This is among the best-replicated findings in the judgement literature, and separating the two lines is the cheapest known defence.
04Model people by their constraintsB
Not what they say. What they are judged on, what they fear, what they run out of.
Three questions per person. What is this person evaluated on by someone above them? What would embarrass them in front of the people they care about? What do they run out of first — time, patience, political cover, attention? Predictions built from constraints hold; predictions built from stated intentions do not.
Then write the prediction down before the event. One line: what they will do and why. One line afterwards: what they did. Calibration improves with feedback and not otherwise; intuition about people is only trustworthy in environments where you find out quickly whether you were right. The log is what makes this one of those environments.
05Find the structure under the instanceB
Every situation is a shape you have met somewhere else. Name the shape.
This is Boyd's actual method and the thing people mistake for genius. Read widely across fields that have nothing to do with each other, then notice the same structure wearing different clothes. A seminar complaint is a legal standing problem. A staffroom is an authority-versus-power split. A disciplinary process is a punishment regardless of its finding. The vocabulary rotates every generation; the move never changes.
The payoff is that the shape tells you what the situation needs in order to work — and therefore what to remove. A complaint that borrows an absent person's voice needs the person to stay absent. A free rider needs the contribution to stay invisible. You do not have to find who; you have to find what it runs on. A hunt for who will always find somebody. The pattern catalogue below is the starting set.
06State the objective as a checkable conditionA
Not "handle it well." Make it short. Make him expensive. Change the question.
The test is whether an outsider could tell afterwards if you hit it. "Make it eleven days instead of eleven weeks" passes. "Win" does not, because winning is a feeling and you will award it to yourself. Specific, checkable objectives outperform vague ones by a wide and well-replicated margin, and the Aim tab will not let you save one without the test.
Then ask what the cheapest action is that satisfies the condition. Usually it is smaller than the one you were about to take, and usually it involves not doing something.
07Decide the hard things in calmA
Three things on the bus. One of them is what you will not do.
By the time you are deciding in the room, you have lost. Before any hard conversation, write three things: what you will concede immediately because it is true and free; what you will ask for; what you will not do no matter how available it is. The last one is the one that costs, and it is not a thinking skill — it is a decision you make in advance and then find out whether you meant.
The evidence here is unusually strong. If–then plans made in advance roughly double the rate at which people do the hard thing in the moment, across a very large number of studies, because the decision has already been made and the moment only has to recognise it.
08Act small, early, visibly usefulB
Goodwill is cheapest bought in week three with a solved physical problem.
Nobody can be impressive in week three; they have not done anything yet. What a newcomer can do is solve one small physical problem that everybody has and nobody has measured: the bus, the photocopier, the room booking, the thing that wastes ten minutes a day. Physical problems are the right kind because a solution has no status content; it cannot be judged wrong or read as a bid.
The obligation created is out of all proportion to the favour, which is the oldest finding in social exchange. It only works because the favour is real.
09Review against the predictionA
Reflection without a prior prediction is unfalsifiable. It confirms whatever you already believe about yourself.
This is the whole difference between action research and a diary. What did I expect, what happened, where was the gap, what do I change — and is that a lesson or an excuse. Without the written prediction the review has nothing to be wrong against, which is why journalling makes people feel better and change nothing.
Record being wrong with the same weight as being right. Six weeks mispronouncing a name. Four days convicting the wrong person on a beautiful case. The error is the data; the win is just the absence of it.
10One move past the obvious responseB
For any action: what does the other party do in reply, and what does that leave me?
This is as far ahead as he ever actually looks. Not five moves. One. Before acting, write what the other side does in response and whether that position is better or worse than the one you are in. Most bad moves fail this test immediately and most people never run it.
Its sharper form is second intention, borrowed from fencing: a move you expect to fail, made to draw the response you can actually use. And its defensive form is the premortem — assume the plan has failed, then explain why. Imagining an outcome as already having happened measurably improves the reasons people generate for it.
How to be more observant
Where to put your eyes
Arrive early. The five minutes before a meeting starts carry more information than the meeting: who sits with whom, who checks with whom before speaking, who the latecomers apologise to.
Watch eyes when a hard question lands. Before anyone answers, the room looks at someone. That is who holds authority, and it is frequently not the person running the meeting.
Count. Who spoke, how many times, in what order, who spoke after whom. Order of speaking is a deference map and nobody notices you building it.
Note how disagreements end. Every room has a de-escalation signal — a phrase that means I am withdrawing before this becomes a scene. Learn it. It tells you where the real limits are.
Note what people apologise for. That is the unwritten rule, stated out loud by the person breaking it.
Note what gets praised versus what gets rewarded. They differ in every institution, and the gap is the institution's actual values.
Note the number everyone quotes. Then measure it.
Ask one insider one naive question a week. What's the thing everybody here knows that nobody says out loud? An eighteen-year-old answered that in nine words. It costs a coffee.
Write it within the hour or lose it. One line. Observation only. Meaning goes in the second field, later, if at all.
Patterns to watch for
The starting catalogue
Authority / power split. The one the room believes is not the one who decides. Unstable; watch for the day one needs something only the other has.
The borrowed voice. Someone speaks for an absent party to gain something for themselves. Ask who has standing. The complaint needs the absent person to stay absent.
The process is the punishment. A procedure whose cost is paid regardless of outcome. The only variable you control is length.
The invisible contribution. Free riding happens where effort cannot be seen. Fix with visibility, not rules.
The assumed number. A figure everyone quotes and nobody has measured.
The identity objection. A refusal about self-image rather than the proposal. Predictable, finite, collapses when named.
Cheap to lose. A person or thing the institution can drop at no cost. The move is to make it expensive, not to defend it.
The unwinnable question. The question on the table has no good answer for you. Find the question nobody has to ask.
Method instead of objective. Someone has been told how to walk up the hill but not what the hill is for. They stop thinking the moment they are confused.
Praised versus rewarded. What the institution says it values and what it actually pays for.
The culminating point. A win pushed one step past where it was complete, which is where it turns into a loss.
Moral isolation. Menace, uncertainty and mistrust around one person. When a room goes quiet on someone, that is a mechanism, not a mood.
The explanation as confession. Length reads as anxiety; anxiety reads as guilt. The long defence is the admission.
Add your own as they turn up. A pattern earns its place in the catalogue when you have seen it in two different domains.

Ordered by cost. The cheap ones first because they generate the evidence that makes the slow ones worth sticking with. Skill 5 is years, not a fortnight; the gate is the start of it, not the end.
Why not affirmations
Skipped on the evidence B. Reaffirming your values under threat has real support. Repeating a statement about yourself does not, and for people who do not already believe it, positive self-statements made them feel measurably worse. The Boyd equivalent is not a sentence about who you are. It is a standing decision about what you will do, written down before the moment, which has the strongest evidence on this page.

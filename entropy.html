The Entropy Protocol

Left alone, things drift from rare arrangements to common ones. You cannot stop the drift. You can decide what it drifts toward, what it costs you, how fast it runs, and what it powers on the way.

The physics
The deckA
A new deck sits in exactly one sorted order. It can sit in about 8 × 1067 orders in total — a number with 68 digits. Shuffle it and it lands in a mixed-up order, not because shuffling prefers mess, but because almost every possible order is a mixed-up one.
Entropy is a count of arrangements. Specifically: how many microscopic arrangements look the same from the outside. “Sorted” has one. “Mixed” has nearly all of them. Boltzmann wrote it as S = k log W, and it is carved on his tombstone.
Shake the box
Sixteen coins. There is one way to get sixteen heads and 12,870 ways to get eight. Shake it and see where it lands.
Every coin starts heads — the one rare arrangement.
Direction, then stillnessA
Change has a direction. Ink spreads through water and never gathers back into a drop. Coffee cools to the room and never reheats. Nothing forbids the reverse; it is just so outnumbered that it never happens. That one-way drift is the second law of thermodynamics, and it is the only law of physics that tells past from future.
Then the drift stops. Once something reaches its most common arrangement — ink fully spread, coffee at room temperature — nothing you would notice changes any more. That is equilibrium. Entropy is not endless change; it is why change has a direction and why it runs out.
Order is possible, at a price. A fridge, a body, a tidy room: each holds a rare arrangement by spending energy, and pays for it by creating more disorder somewhere else. Local order is always bought, never free.
Where the physics ends. Everything below this line is analogy. People, classrooms and habits are not thermodynamic systems, and “entropy says” is never an argument for a life decision. The pattern carries over well — left alone, things drift toward whatever is easiest and most available — so each move is graded on its own behavioural evidence, not on borrowed authority from physics.
The four moves
1Make the good outcome the likely oneA
The cheapest way to get an outcome is not to push for it every time. It is to arrange things so that it is what happens when nobody pushes.
Use when you want something to happen repeatedly, or you have pushed for the same thing twice.
Set the default — decide once, in advance, so the good option is what happens if you do nothing.
Move the cue into reach and the rival out of it — whatever is closest wins most of the time.
Shorten the path — count the steps to the good option and the steps to the bad one. Make the first number smaller.
Room 148 Warm-up on screen and books on desks before the bell. The easiest thing to do on arrival is start.
Home Phone charges in another room during time with the girls. Being present becomes the default instead of an act of will.
Health Blood work booked as a yearly recurring appointment. Things with no default never happen, however much you mean to.
Income The weekly project block sits in the calendar as a fixed slot, not as something you find time for.
2Count the upkeepC
Everything organised costs energy to stay organised, forever. Every new thing you start is a permanent bill, not a one-time purchase.
Use when you are about to start anything new — a build, a commitment, a system, a protocol.
Price the year, not the evening — ask what it will cost to keep alive in twelve months, not what it costs to make tonight.
One in, one out — name what retires to make room before you start.
Maintain small and often — a weekly tidy costs far less than a rescue after a month of drift.
Forge OS The blank Drill tab sat in eight protocol pages for weeks before anyone noticed. Nothing broke it; nobody was paying the upkeep.
Room 148 Routines taught in September drift after every break. Re-teaching them in the first class back is upkeep, not repetition.
Home Ten minutes of reset each evening beats the Saturday rescue.
This page is itself an addition. It earns its place only if it replaces pushing with design. Otherwise it is one more thing to maintain.
3Slow the drifts you can’t stopA
Some declines are certain but slow, and their speed is negotiable. You will not stop them. You can decide how long they take.
Use when a decline is gradual and inevitable — the kind nobody notices month to month.
Watch the rate, not just the direction — “it is declining” is fixed; “how fast” is the part you control.
Put a small, regular brake on it — many little applications beat one large effort later.
Start before it shows — by the time a slow drift is visible, most of it has already happened.
Muscle Loss after 40 is certain; its pace is not. Lifting three days and protein around 1.6 g/kg on eating days are the brake.
Fitness Cardiorespiratory fitness falls with age. Aerobic volume is what slows it, which is why cardio minutes matter as much as lifting.
Friends Closeness fades with time apart. Small regular contact slows it far more cheaply than rebuilding a friendship that has lapsed.
Blood Markers like ApoB and blood pressure drift silently for years. A yearly check sees the rate while it is still cheap to change.
4Harness drifts already flowingB
Most useful work in the world is drift being channelled — water running downhill through a wheel. Fight a strong tendency only when it cannot be pointed somewhere useful.
Use when you keep fighting the same tendency, in yourself or in a room.
Find which way it already runs — name the tendency without judging it.
Attach what you want to it — “when X happens, I will Y,” riding on something that already occurs.
Fight only what cannot be channelled — some drifts, like the phone during family time, have no useful direction. Those get Move 1 instead.
Room 148 Students drift toward talking to each other. Structured partner talk turns the thing you would suppress into the lesson.
You You drift toward building. Pointed at low-impact work it is a problem; pointed deliberately at income work (Getting Paid) it is the engine of the exit.
Walks Listening to protocols on walks already rides this: the walk happens anyway, and the learning is carried on it.
Move 1 overlaps with the Friction Engineering protocol in your terrain doctrine document. That one goes deeper on physical set-up; this one is the reason it works.
The tools
The drift map10 min · once, then each quarter
Write down three drifts working against you and three working for you. Beside each, name the move it needs: 1 to redesign, 2 to price, 3 to slow, 4 to harness.
What slides every time I stop paying attention to it?
What do I keep pushing for that never sticks?
What happens easily that I could point somewhere better?
Pushed twicestanding rule
If you have pushed for the same outcome twice, the third time is a redesign, not a push. Reminding, resolving and trying harder are all pushes. Changing the conditions is the only thing that counts as the third move.
The upkeep checkbefore any new build
What does this cost each week to keep alive?
What breaks, quietly, if I stop paying that?
What am I retiring to make room for it?
No answer to the third question means it waits for Sunday.
The weekly drift audit2 questions · Sunday review
What did I have to push for more than once this week?
What slid without my noticing?
The first feeds Pushed twice. The second feeds Move 3. Folded into the existing Sunday review, with nothing new to track.
The One Thing
When you have pushed for something twice, stop pushing and redesign the conditions.
Everything else here supports that one move. Effort spent holding something uphill is effort spent forever. Effort spent reshaping the slope is spent once.
Failure modes
Redesign as displacement — endlessly engineering the environment instead of doing the work it was meant to make easy. The redesign is a build, and builds are your known drift.
Analogy overreach — treating “entropy” as proof. It is a lens for spotting patterns, never evidence that a pattern exists.
Defaults decay too — a system set up once erodes like anything else. The weekly audit is what catches it.
Harnessing what should be blocked — some drifts have no useful direction. Trying to “channel” the phone during family time just keeps it in the room.
Upkeep as an excuse — counting the cost is not a reason to never start. Some things are worth their bill. The point is to know the bill before signing.
Evidence
A Strong. The physics itself: Boltzmann's statistical entropy and the second law. The deck count (52! ≈ 8.07 × 1067) and the coin counts were computed, not recalled. Defaults: Johnson & Goldstein (2003) on organ-donor defaults; Madrian & Shea (2001) on automatic pension enrolment. Resistance training and adequate protein slowing age-related muscle loss. Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis) for the “when X, I will Y” step.

B Good, with variance. Habits as context-cued behaviour (Wood & Neal; Wood & Rünger). Temptation bundling (Milkman et al. 2014), the closest tested version of harnessing a drift. Friendship closeness decaying with reduced contact (Roberts & Dunbar).

C Extrapolated. The four-move frame is an organising lens, not a tested model. “Count the upkeep” borrows from planning-fallacy research, which is about underestimating time to finish, not ongoing cost. The extension is reasonable and untested.

D Arbitrary. “Twice”, the audit questions, one in one out, and the quarterly drift map. No evidence sets these. They are fixed in advance so they cannot be renegotiated in the moment.

Deliberately excluded: “life is a battle against entropy” and claims that entropy explains ageing or decline. As a physical explanation that is wrong; living things are open systems. Also excluded: the popular “20-second rule” for friction, which has little direct testing behind the number.

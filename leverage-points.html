Leverage Points

Twelve places to intervene in a system, weakest to strongest, after Donella Meadows (1999). People find the right point by instinct and push it the wrong way, and spend nearly all their effort at the bottom of the list. Four instruments in the Work tab.

The bathtub
Stocks, flows, and the invisible goalDOCTRINE
Everything in the essay rests on this one picture.
A stock is anything that accumulates: money, trust with a student, how rested you are, how many people know your name.
Stocks change only through flows — the faucet and the drain. So they move slowly, and a change in behaviour takes weeks to show in a result.
The goal, the rules and the information that decide how the taps get turned are invisible. You can see the water. You usually cannot see why it moves.
Hold onto the shower with the boiler four floors down. It comes back at lever 9.

Rung one · the physical levers
12Numbers and parametersDOCTRINE
The settings on the taps. The weakest lever, and where almost all attention goes.
Change a number and a stagnant system rarely starts, a wild one rarely steadies, a runaway one rarely brakes. New hands on the same old taps change little. Her landlord story: weeks hunting for the perfectly fair rent, when there was a wide grey zone where both sides did fine. Stop worrying and get on with your life.
The exception: a number matters when it sits near a threshold that switches on something higher up. Ask "is this number near a threshold?" — most are not.
Refining the same number for the third time is the signal to climb a rung. Same rule as Entropy: pushed twice means redesign.
Forge OSThe 180-minute floor, ten minutes of mushin, the sleep-alert threshold. Careful work — did any of it change what you do, or how it reads?
Room 148Moving a due date a day, ten quiz items to eight. Sometimes right; rarely what unsticks a class.
11BuffersDOCTRINEC
A stock big relative to its flows. A lake, not a river.
Rivers flood; lakes absorb. Bigger buffers steady a system, but too big makes it stiff and slow — which is why businesses went just-in-time. Usually physical and hard to change, so low on the list.
Term time is a river: about two free hours a week, and one sick kid or marking crunch floods it. Summer is the lake. The lever is not willpower — it is stop scheduling river-time as if it were lake-time. Continuity work goes to summer; term gets small, interruptible pieces.
Room 148The time-fillers library is a buffer you already built — it absorbs a collapsed block so you don't have to.
IncomeThe exit number on the brief is a buffer: the stock that must exist before leaving is safe.
Too bigPrepping five units ahead feels safe and locks you in when the class needs something else.
C for the related scarcity research: real, but some lab effects have not replicated.
10Physical structureDOCTRINE
The plumbing: what connects to what, and through which bottleneck.
Hungary's roads all ran through central Budapest; no traffic light could fix the result. Structure is powerful and slow to change. The leverage is in good design at the start, then in respecting the bottlenecks.
Your structure — two jobs, three dependents, Tue/Thu nights, Room 148's footprint — will not move because you want it to. Change it at the moments you know are coming: September, a course launch, the start of a unit's seating plan.
Rung two · timing and loops
09DelaysDOCTRINEA
The London hotel shower: a minute between the tap and the water. Scalding, freezing, scalding.
Late feedback makes you overshoot and undershoot. Sterman's Beer Distribution Game: with a few weeks' shipping delay, nearly everyone — experienced managers included — over-orders then under-orders, because they ignore what is already in the pipe. A for the human failure.
Delays are often unchangeable, so the usual move is to slow your own rate of change to match them, not wish them away.
IncomeSelf-Evidence renamed, restructured and partly rebuilt in a couple of months, with no market signal back yet. That is turning the tap before the water arrives. Shorten the delay (real people, one module) and slow the rebuilds.
Room 148A report card is a delay of weeks; an exit ticket is minutes. The exit ticket is why you can correct in September instead of finding out in November.
HealthJudging a new habit after four days when its effects take three weeks is the shower.
08Balancing loopsDOCTRINEB
Thermostats. Goal, gap-measurer, response.
Strength only matters relative to the load. The thermostat that holds on a cold day fails with every window open. A Sunday review set at August strength may be underpowered in mid-October.
Her most quotable warning: we strip out emergency correction loops because they look costly and rarely fire — and she names our time for rest, recreation, socialising and meditation. Nothing happens that week. Over time, the range of conditions you can survive narrows sharply.
Protect them hardest exactly when they seem least necessary; strengthen them when the load rises.
YoursSunday review, "not relevant", mushin, sleep, sauna, unscored time with your daughters.
Room 148A predictable opening routine pulls a class back to working order at no cost — you see its value on the day you skip it.
07Reinforcing loopsDOCTRINEA
The more it runs, the more power it has to run. Every growth and every collapse.
Slowing a runaway loop beats strengthening the brakes against it — ease off the accelerator rather than ask for better brakes. And watch "success to the successful": winners get the means to win again.
Build loopIdea → build → three more ideas. Parking lot, Sunday review and the three-tier rule are brakes. Lowering the gain does more: a new build retires an old one, nothing starts mid-term.
IncomeThe audience loop — readers who share and bring readers — is not switched on. Substack is paused. It has not started compounding.
HomeTime that is genuinely theirs → trust → they bring you more → the time is worth more. It runs downward too.
Room 148The confident speaker answers first, practises more, gets more confident. Cold-call, think-pair-share and wait time lower that gain.
Rung three · information and rules
06Information flowsAD
Who sees what. Where leverage starts to get cheap.
Identical houses; some had the electric meter in the basement, some in the front hall. The front-hall homes used 30% less. Honest note: the 30% is a story she passes on (D). Controlled trials point the same way, smaller: reviews such as Darby (2006) put direct feedback at roughly 5–15%; a German field study found about 4.5%; Opower's neighbour-comparison reports about 2% across hundreds of thousands of homes. Feedback works — A.
Stronger: the US Toxic Release Inventory (1986) required factories to publish emissions — no fines, no new limits. Emissions fell about 40% in four years, by her account.
Missing feedback is one of the commonest causes of a broken system, and restoring it is far cheaper than rebuilding. It must go to the place the decision is made, in a form that compels: put the town's water intake downstream of its own outflow.
DoneThe exit number on the brief and the video audit are both front-hall meters. You know how to pull this lever.
BasementSelf-Evidence: rich data on you, almost none from the people it is for. Nobody's reaction to Module 1 reaches your front hall.
Room 148A visible tally of who has spoken today is a meter for them and for you.
05RulesB
Boundaries, incentives, punishments — and whoever writes them.
Her college thought experiment: students grade teachers; no degrees, you leave when you've learned it; a whole class graded as one. Imagine behaviour under each and you feel how much rules decide. Research version: defaults — Johnson & Goldstein (2003), opt-out donation countries register far more donors. Effect on actual donations smaller; core finding holds.
Yours work because they take the decision out of the moment: the three-second rule, "the hard line wins", "pushed twice means redesign".
Two rules that would beat effort: no new module, page or build until N real people have been through Module 1. And a new build only starts by retiring an old one, never mid-term.
Room 148How work is marked, what counts, whether a good copy can be resubmitted — change the grading rule and behaviour moves more than any instruction would.
Rung four · design and goals
04Self-organizationDOCTRINEC
The power to change your own structure. Needs variety plus a way to select.
Not magic: a stock of variety and a test for what works. Any system that wipes out its own variety and scorns experiment is doomed in the long run.
Action research is this lever — plan, act, observe, reflect generates variety and selects by measuring. It is your most native lever and the product you sell.
Two warnings. Selection needs outside data, or the loop drifts on its own readings. And nearly every idea becoming a protocol and an HTML page is a single culture. Sometimes the right answer is a conversation, a decision not to build, or a test with five strangers.
03GoalsADOCTRINE
Read the goal from what the system does, not what it says.
Change the goal and everything below bends to it. Corporations say profit; their behaviour says grow and control. And this is the one rung where a single person changes the whole system — by stating, repeating and insisting on a new goal. In a household or a classroom, that person is just whoever is in charge.
Stated: more time with family; exiting teaching is the means. Read from behaviour: what would someone say your goal was, watching a month of your discretionary hours without the explanation?
Exiting teaching is one route to family time, not the only one and maybe not the fastest. Give the others a fair hearing or the means stands in for the end for years.
A for goal-setting (specific goals direct effort). The behaviour-reading is doctrine — the same gap Argyris calls espoused theory vs theory-in-use.
Rung five · the mind
02The paradigmDOCTRINEC
The assumptions too deep to bother saying. Goals, rules and loops all grow out of them.
Hardest to change in a society; for one person it can happen in a moment. Kuhn's method: keep pointing at the anomalies the old paradigm cannot explain.
Under much of your system: the narrative of catching up and not yet being enough, and a second paradigm that every part of life can be optimized. They have built real things. They are still paradigms, and they decide which problems you notice.
You already found the anomalies: "not relevant", and the IMMERSE tag — a deliberate place where you refuse to measure.
Room 148"I'm bad at English" drives everything downstream. A pep talk rarely moves it; an anomaly does — a paragraph they wrote that is undeniably good.
01Transcending paradigmsDOCTRINE
No paradigm is true — including yours — and seeing that is funny, not frightening.
If none is true, you can choose whichever serves your purpose. "Not relevant" worked where years of labelling did not because it stepped outside the frame instead of arguing inside it. Mushin is the daily practice of the same. Nothing to build here. It is practised, not engineered.
Her final caution. The higher the lever, the harder the system resists — resistance is a sign you are near something real. No cheap tickets to mastery. Her last line: power has less to do with pushing leverage points than with strategically, profoundly letting go.
How to spot one
1
Policy resistance. Pushing harder, nothing moves → you are on a parameter. Climb.
2
Oscillation. Swings, or rebuilding the same thing → look for a delay. Shorten it or slow down.
3
Missing meter. What would change your behaviour if you saw it daily — and does it reach you?
4
Goal–behaviour gap. Stated goal vs the goal the hours actually serve.
5
Direction. Would your obvious move speed up the loop you are trying to slow? Building a tool to manage your projects is the build loop pushed the wrong way.
Map a domain
The five signals are for a problem that is already stuck. This is for a different question: where is the leverage in a whole part of your life, when nothing is obviously broken? Use the Map a domain tool in the Work tab.
Four realms, not twelve leversDOCTRINE
Abson and colleagues (2017) grouped her twelve into four, shallow to deep. Easier to carry on a walk; little is lost.
Parameters — numbers, buffers, physical structure (12–10).
Feedbacks — delays and loops (9–7).
Design — information flows, rules, self-organization (6–4).
Intent — the goal, the paradigm, stepping outside it (3–1).
The ranking is untested. A 2020 review of 301 real interventions (Dorninger and colleagues) found most worked on shallow levers B — that shows where people push, not that deeper works better. Treat the order as a strong hypothesis. Sometimes a shallow change is the only way in to a deep one.
The five steps
1
Name the stock. What builds up or drains away here — trust, sleep, a quiet student’s confidence, strangers who know your work. No stock, no system.
2
Count before you change. A baseline number for the stock, or a stand-in for it, before you touch anything. Your own Self-Evidence rule.
3
Ask deepest first. Intent, then design, then feedbacks, then parameters. Deep questions cost nothing to ask — only to act on.
4
Choose by cost to test, not by rank. The deepest rung you can test in two weeks on about two hours a week. A rule you can change on Monday beats a paradigm you can only think about.
5
One variable, then reverse it. Check direction first (Forrester). Then A, B, A, B — if the stock follows both ways, it is real.
Steps 2 and 5 are action research and the single-case reversal design B. Steps 1, 3 and 4 are reasoning.
The questions, deepest first
Intent
If someone saw only where my hours go here, what would they say I am trying to achieve?
What do I believe about this domain that, if false, would change everything I do here?
Whose goal is this, really — mine, someone else’s, or an older version of me?
Design
What rule, written or just habit, produces the result I keep getting?
Who doesn’t see something that would change their behaviour if they saw it daily — me included?
Who is allowed to change the rules here, and would it help to let more people?
Feedbacks
What is feeding on itself here, upwards or downwards?
How long between acting and knowing whether it worked?
Is anything swinging back and forth, or am I rebuilding the same thing again?
Parameters
Where is there no slack, so one bad day sinks the plan?
What fixed structure am I fighting instead of designing around?
Which number have I already tuned three times? (If one, you are probably on the wrong rung.)
Nine domains
The core questions plus the ones that matter most in each. The stock is a suggestion; the one you name is the one that counts. All reasoning, not measured.
01Family
Stock: your daughters’ sense that your attention, when they have it, is really theirs.
What does each of them actually see of my attention on an ordinary weeknight — and what would a week’s tally show?
What rule decides who gets my evening: the phone in the room, a build running, marking first?
Rules beat willpower here — evening is when patience runs lowest. Map your own part; don’t diagnose them.
02Classroom
Stock: a student’s skill or confidence, built a little each day.
Which “success to the successful” loop is running in each block, and who is on the losing end?
What don’t students see about their own progress that would change their effort if they saw it daily?
You already work the rules rung here: cold calling and wait time are rule changes.
03Night class
Stock: adult learners’ energy and willingness to speak after a full day.
What rule of the room decides who talks and who listens?
How long between a lesson and a student using the language outside class — and do I ever hear about it?
04Income
Stock: people outside your house who know your work and would pay for it.
Is anything flowing into that stock right now?
How long between building something and a stranger reacting — and is the work feeding this stock or the build loop?
05Build
Stock: live builds you are maintaining. The build loop gets its own map.
What rule decides whether a new build starts?
Does anything ever leave, or does it only grow?
Reduce the gain, not add brakes: a new build retires an old one.
06Health
Stock: sleep, or fitness built over months.
Which number would change my choice if I saw it at the moment of choosing, not the next morning?
Where is a delay hiding a result? Training takes weeks to show A — easy to quit before it has worked.
07Money
Stock: capital in your existing money systems.
What decides when I act — a rule, or a mood?
How long between a decision and an honest verdict? Rebuilding a strategy every few weeks is the shower swinging.
08Friends
Stock: people you would call if something went wrong, and how recently you spoke.
What rule, or lack of one, decides when I get in touch?
No hours go here during term unless a rule gives it some.
09Mind
Stock: your attention, and what fills it.
What story about myself would I drop if it cost me something I could see?
Where did the changes that actually stuck come from — and which rung were they on?

Weekly practice
Inside the existing Sunday review, not a new ritual: one stuck problem through the Lever Audit in the Work tab. What accumulates, what fills it, what drains it, how long the feedback takes, which lever you are pushing by number — then name what one rung up would look like. You don't have to act on it. Failure modes
Climbing for its own sake. Paradigm talk flatters more than changing a deadline. If it is a parameter, change it and move on.
Mapping instead of moving. If the audit takes longer than the fix, it has become the problem.
Pushing the right lever backwards. Forrester's warning, and the commonest failure. Check the direction.
Running it on people. Diagnosing your daughters', students' or colleagues' paradigms feels clever and damages relationships. Systems you are inside, and yourself.
Expecting high levers to be easy. Resistance is a sign you are close, not wrong.
Hands off. Entropy owns "pushed twice means redesign" and upkeep. Argyris owns the say/do gap in depth. Shaping and Adapting owns predictions and the outside judge. This page is the map of where to push; those are the tools once you know.
Evidence key
A — replicated experiments or meta-analysis, effect holds.
B — good evidence, some contest at the edges.
C — coherent and widely used, thin controlled testing.
D — a story or case report; useful, unmeasured.
DOCTRINE — systems reasoning from modelling and long practice; a strong framework never measured as a whole.

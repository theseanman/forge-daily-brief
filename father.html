The Father Protocol

The exit is being built for this. Two daughters, 10 and 14, on opposite sides of a real line in the research, and a week with narrow windows. This is what to do and say inside those windows — and what the evidence actually supports.

Change
Read this first. Almost everything here is correlational: families that do X tend to have kids who do better, and part of that is who those families already are. None of it is a guarantee, and nothing on this page makes a child successful on command. What it does is put the odds on the right side — and the moves cost minutes, not money. Each item carries its grade so you know which ones are load-bearing.
The Base Layer · both girls
1Warmth plus structureB
The most replicated finding in parenting research: high warmth together with clear, explained limits. Not strict, not soft — both at once.
Say the warmth out loud — kids do not infer it from effort on their behalf. The exit you are building is invisible to them; the sentence is not.
Explain the limit — a reason given once beats a rule repeated. The rule still holds.
Never use love as leverage — guilt, silent treatment, withdrawn affection. This is “psychological control”, and it is consistently linked to worse outcomes, especially in teenagers.
2The phone out of the roomB
When a parent is on the phone mid-conversation, kids read it as the phone winning. The research calls it phubbing and links it to teen anxiety and low mood.
Two protected windows — the goodbye and the dinner table. Not on the table face-down: out of the room.
When she starts talking, the phone goes down first — before you answer. The gesture is the message.
Consistent association across many studies, but mostly cross-sectional — the direction is not proven. It is cheap enough to act on anyway.
3Rituals that repeatB
Predictable small things carry more than rare big ones. A child who knows it is coming can lean on it.
Three dinners a week, together — the threshold the family-meal research uses. More is better; three is the floor.
Same shape every time — the goodbye, the door check-in, the weekend one-on-one. Do not redesign them. This is Second Nature’s Stable Structure, pointed at home.
4Repair after a ruptureC
Every parent snaps, misses things, gets it wrong. What predicts the relationship is not the absence of ruptures but whether they get repaired.
Go back first — you, not her, and same day where you can. Short: what you did, that it was not okay, what you will do instead.
No “but” — “I’m sorry I yelled, but you…” is a second rupture.
The strongest repair research is on infants; carrying it to 10 and 14 is reasonable extrapolation, not a tested finding.
What to do and say · the evidence-backed moves
The things you asked for: what you can do or say that is linked to them being healthier, happier and more successful. Each has the words to use and the words to avoid.
1Eat together, three-plus times a weekB
Healthier: linked to better nutrition across tens of thousands of children. Happier: linked to higher wellbeing, lower depression and fewer risk behaviours in teens, even after accounting for how close the family already is.
Say: “What was the best part of today, and the worst?” — then answer it yourself too.Not: a debrief of marks, chores and what went wrong. The table is not the office.
2Praise the process, not the personB
Successful: praise for effort and strategy is linked to kids believing ability grows with work — and choosing harder things.
Say: “You kept going when that got hard.” “How did you figure that out?”Not: “You’re so smart.” And not inflated praise — “that’s the best drawing ever” can backfire on a child who already doubts herself.
The famous mindset effects are smaller in large replications than the popular version suggests. Real, modest, and free.
3Make it safe to tell you thingsB
Safer: what parents actually know about a teen’s life comes mostly from what she chooses to tell them, not from asking. Your reaction today decides whether she tells you next time.
Say, first, every time: “Thanks for telling me.” Then ask before advising: “Do you want help with it, or do you just want me to know?”Not: an instant lecture, alarm, or consequences in the first sentence. Deal with the problem later; protect the channel now.
4Hand over choicesB
Happier and more motivated: autonomy-supportive parenting — choices, reasons, her view taken seriously — is linked to better self-motivation and wellbeing.
Say: “What do you think you should do?” “Here’s why I’m asking this.” “You pick: this or that.”Not: “Because I said so” as the default. Limits stay; the reasons come with them.
5Talk about the future, not the homeworkB
Successful: in the research on middle-schoolers, linking school to their own goals is associated with achievement more strongly than helping with homework.
Say: “What would you want to be doing at 25? What would help get you there?”Not: checking every assignment. Stay interested in where she is going; let the homework be hers.
6Protect their sleepB
Healthier and happier: short sleep in teens is tied to low mood and worse school performance, and phones in the bedroom at night cut sleep.
Phones charge outside bedrooms overnight — a house rule, explained, and one you follow too. Your evening protocol already models it.
7Say the specific thing you loveC
General warmth is well supported; the specific phrasing is judgement, not a finding. Specific lands better than general because it proves you were watching.
Say: “I love how you look out for your sister.” “I noticed you stuck up for that kid.”Not: only saying it after a win. Warmth tied to performance becomes a price.
Your 10-year-old
She is still in the window where she will take one-on-one time and ask for more. It closes; this is the time it is cheapest.
AHer timeC
Ten to fifteen minutes, just her, and she leads. You follow, describe what she is doing, and enjoy it. No teaching, no questions quiz, no correcting.
Describe, don’t direct — “You’re building the wall higher.” Narrating tells her you are watching her, not the task.
Label the good thing — “Thanks for showing me how that works.”
This is the core of parent-child interaction therapy, which has strong trial support — but for ages 2 to 7. At 10 it is extrapolation, backed by the broader evidence on warm responsiveness.
BServe and returnB
When she brings you something — a fact, a joke, a drawing — answer the thing she brought, not the thing you were doing.
Turn toward — eyes up, one real response. Ten seconds. The small bids are where the relationship is built.
Your 14-year-old
Child-led play is the wrong model now and would feel patronising. The research here is about autonomy, disclosure and warmth that does not depend on being returned.
ASide by side, not face to faceC
Teenagers talk when nobody is looking at them. Drives, cooking, errands, walking somewhere. The activity is cover for the conversation.
One side-by-side a week, just the two of you — a drive she needs anyway counts.
Don’t fill the silence — the good stuff usually arrives in minute twelve, not minute one.
Shared one-on-one time with fathers in adolescence has been linked to better social skills. From recall — verify before quoting.
BWarmth that doesn’t need a replyB
At 14, pulling away from parents is normal development, not rejection. The teens who do best still have the warmth coming, whether or not they send it back.
Keep offering — the eye-roll is not a verdict on you. Say the thing anyway and don’t ask for anything in return.
Respect the door — knock, and let her privacy be real. It is part of why she tells you things.
The Week Map
Every weekday, 07:37–07:45 · the goodbye. Phone away. Eye contact, one line, and something specific about her day: “Good luck on the quiz — you prepped for it.” Thirty seconds each.
Mon / Wed / Fri evenings · the dinner anchor. Protect three dinners a week together, phone out of the room. Training fits around them where it can.
Tue / Thu after 21:30 · the door check-in. Your 14-year-old is likely still up: five minutes at her door, knock first, no question quota. “How was today?” and then listen.
Tue / Thu · the note. Your 10-year-old may be asleep when you get home, so she gets hers the night before: a short note where she will find it in the morning. Two lines, specific.
Weekends · one-on-one, separately. Her time with your 10-year-old; a side-by-side with your 14-year-old. Separate is the point — each gets you without the other.

The One Thing
Phone out of the room — and when she tells you something, “Thanks for telling me” comes first.
If only one thing happens, it is this. It costs nothing, it works for both ages, and it keeps the channel open for everything else on this page. A 14-year-old who still tells you things is the best early-warning system you will ever have.
Failure Modes
Turning your kids into a project — the most likely one for you. Scoring the hour, optimising the conversation, logging it. That is Second Nature’s improving thought, and it displaces the hour it is trying to improve. Nothing on this page gets tracked.
Interrogation at dinner — questions in a row feel like an audit. One question, then your own answer.
Teaching during her time — the 10-year-old’s time stops being hers the moment it has a lesson in it.
Forcing face-to-face on a 14-year-old — “sit down, we need to talk” closes more than it opens.
Warmth only after wins — she learns the price.
Running the drill mid-moment — the Drill tab is review-only. Never during.
Evidence
B Good, consistent, mostly correlational. Family meals: a 2011 Pediatrics meta-analysis of 17 studies (~183,000 children), and a 2013 New Zealand study linking frequent meals to teen wellbeing after controlling for family connectedness. Disclosure: Stattin & Kerr (2000) and replications — parental knowledge tracks what teens tell, more than what parents ask. Process praise: Gunderson et al. (2013), a naturalistic follow-up over five years; Mueller & Dweck (1998) in the lab. Phubbing: a 2024 systematic review found every included study linked it to teen anxiety or depression, mostly cross-sectional. Authoritative parenting, psychological control, autonomy support, academic socialisation (Hill & Tyson 2009) and teen sleep: large literatures, from recall.

C Extrapolated or judgement. PCIT-style child-led time at 10 (trialled at 2–7); repair research carried from infancy; side-by-side talk; specific over general warmth.

D Arbitrary. Every dose and minute count on the ladder. Set in advance so they don’t get renegotiated.

Deliberately excluded: anything promising a smarter or more successful child from a single technique — brain-training, “Mozart”, rigid screen-minute targets. None of them hold up.

Verify before quoting. The meals, disclosure, praise and phubbing sources were checked this session; the rest are from recall.

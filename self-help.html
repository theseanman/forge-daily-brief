The Self-Help Audit

Thirty best-sellers on trial, and the twenty-five things worth keeping. Ranked by evidence × effect × cheapness — which is why the order will surprise you.

How to read this
A Multiple meta-analyses or large pre-registered replications agree, and the effect is real in ordinary people — not just undergraduates for one afternoon.
B Good randomised trials exist and mostly point the same way, but the effect is small, or conditional, or the field has not been through a replication stress test.
C The mechanism is plausible and there is correlational or partial support, but nobody has shown the intervention causes the outcome.
D Tested and failed, or never tested and built on stories.
Return on investment here is three things multiplied: how strong the evidence is, how big the effect is, and how cheap it is for you in time, money and willpower. A Grade A tactic costing an hour a day can lose to a Grade B tactic costing thirty seconds.
The single most important pattern in the whole audit: the practice often works even when the theory is wrong. Napoleon Hill’s theory of the universe is nonsense, but a man who writes down a specific goal is doing something the goal-setting literature supports. One-percent compounding is a metaphor, but habit stacking is an implementation intention. A low grade below is a grade on the book’s theory. The tactics are graded separately.
The thirty, and the verdicts
A reconstruction from publisher-reported sales, which are marketing numbers. Swap a few titles and the twenty-five tactics barely change, because the same handful of ideas recur across the genre.
The five that come out well
Atomic Habits (Clear) B
Compounding is a metaphor; the tactics are real. Habit stacking is an implementation intention. Grade A parts inside a Grade B book.
Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman) A D
Anchoring, loss aversion, framing, base-rate neglect, the planning fallacy all replicate. The priming chapter did not, and Kahneman said so publicly in 2017.
Influence (Cialdini) A
The six principles hold up in field experiments, some enormous. The later behavioural-priming flourishes are the weak part.
Man’s Search for Meaning (Frankl) B C
The memoir is the value. Meaning correlates strongly with wellbeing and with survival in older adults; logotherapy as a therapy has thin trial evidence.
Deep Work (Newport) B
Task-switching costs and attention residue are real and replicated. That deep work is the decisive skill of the century is an opinion.
The seven that are half right
The Power of Habit (Duhigg) B D
Cue, routine, reward is a fair summary. The willpower-as-a-muscle chapter rests on ego depletion, which failed a twenty-three-lab replication in 2016.
Mindset (Dweck) C
The largest meta-analyses find effects on achievement around a tenth of a standard deviation, mostly in students already behind. Nothing like the book’s claims.
Grit (Duckworth) C
The 2017 meta-analysis found grit correlates around point eight with conscientiousness. The perseverance half predicts; the passion half does not.
Outliers (Gladwell) C
Ericsson said Gladwell misread him. Deliberate practice explains about a quarter of the variance in games and music, almost none in professions.
Emotional Intelligence (Goleman) C
EI predicts job performance modestly and adds little once personality and IQ are controlled. That it matters more than IQ is unsupported.
The 7 Habits (Covey) C
Almost none of it tested as written, but “begin with the end in mind” is goal-setting and “seek first to understand” is active listening. Grade B tactics buried in it.
How to Win Friends and Influence People (Carnegie) B
Never tested directly, but person-perception research supports the core: warmth is judged before competence, and criticism triggers defensiveness.
The six that are mostly framing
The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck (Manson) C
Repackaged Stoicism and acceptance therapy. Values clarification has some support; the rest is attitude.
Daring Greatly (Brown) C
Her own work is qualitative. The adjacent self-compassion research is decent, with moderate effects on wellbeing.
Start With Why (Sinek) D C
The golden-circle neuroscience is invented. But a clear purpose does predict engagement in organisations.
The 4-Hour Workweek (Ferriss) C D
Batching and Pareto are engineering heuristics, not findings. The lifestyle claims are one man’s anecdote.
The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up (Kondo) C
Almost no direct research. Clutter correlates with cortisol in women, and a tidy environment reduces the number of decisions.
The Power of Now (Tolle) C B
The book is metaphysics. The practice it points at, present-moment attention, has meta-analytic support at small-to-moderate effect sizes.
The twelve that fail
Think and Grow Rich (Hill) D
No evidence for any of it, and the claimed meetings with Carnegie and Edison have been investigated and found to be fabrications.
The Secret (Byrne) D
Worse than useless. Vividly imagining a result as already achieved reduces effort and outcomes — weight loss, dating, surgery recovery, job hunting.
The Power of Positive Thinking (Peale) D
Same problem. Optimism as a trait correlates with health; positive fantasising as a practice does harm.
Awaken the Giant Within (Robbins) D
Built on neuro-linguistic programming, which has been tested and does not work. The state-change tricks are stagecraft.
Rich Dad Poor Dad (Kiyosaki) D
Rich Dad appears not to have existed, and the leveraged-real-estate advice is the opposite of the evidence. One salvage: the asset-liability lens.
The Magic of Thinking Big (Schwartz) D
Untested motivational prose.
The Power of Your Subconscious Mind (Murphy) D
Untested and unfalsifiable.
See You at the Top (Ziglar) D
The same.
The 5 Love Languages (Chapman) D
A 2024 review found no support: no primary language, the five are not distinct, matching does not predict quality. Keep only the reflex of asking.
Getting Things Done (Allen) C
Never tested and heavy. One piece has support: writing down an unfinished task stops it intruding. The salvage is one tactic, not the system.
12 Rules for Life (Peterson) D
Aphorism and argument. Standing up straight is not one of the supported ones — the power-posing research collapsed. Not harmful.
The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership (Maxwell) D
Twenty-one untested assertions.
The twenty-five, ranked by return
1If-then plansA
From Atomic Habits as habit stacking, and the mechanism inside most of the books that work.
The best-supported tactic in the genre and nearly free. If situation X happens, then I will do Y. The 2006 meta-analysis of ninety-four experiments found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, around two-thirds of a standard deviation; a 2024 update across six hundred and forty-two tests confirmed it holds. It works because it hands the decision to the situation instead of to your willpower in the moment.
Run it: write the trigger before the action. “When I sit down at my desk in 148 after lunch, I open the marking pile before I open email.” A real recurring moment, never a mood. One plan per behaviour.
Cost — two minutes to write, zero to run.
2Mental contrasting: the wish, then the obstacleB
From nowhere in the thirty, because it is the corrective to The Secret and Peale.
Oettingen’s lab spent twenty years showing pure positive visualisation lowers effort and results. The fix is to imagine the wish, then the single biggest thing inside you in the way, in that order. Meta-analysis of the combined package finds a small-to-medium effect across health, academic and personal goals.
Run it: four lines. Wish. Best outcome, felt vividly. The main inner obstacle, named honestly. Then the if-then for that obstacle. Use it on anything you catch yourself daydreaming about.
Cost — five minutes, and it may tell you the goal is not feasible. That is the point.
3Design the environment, do not fight itA
From Atomic Habits, The Power of Habit and Kondo — and it is the whole of your Entropy protocol.
Since Wendy Wood’s work the habit literature is unanimous: habits are cued by context, not intention, and changing the context is more reliable than changing the person. People who moved house changed their exercise habits without trying. Removing one click of friction changes consumption measurably.
Run it: add one step of friction to the competitor and remove one from the target. Phone charges in another room; the book is on the pillow; the marking pile is on the chair you sit in.
Cost — one rearrangement, then zero.
4Make the first step trivially smallB
From Atomic Habits, the two-minute rule.
No meta-analysis of the rule as such, but it rides on two supported findings: starting costs dominate persistence, and the intention-behaviour gap is mostly a failure to start. The 2010 habit study everyone quotes found a median of sixty-six days to automaticity, range eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four, and missing one day did not reset it.
Run it: define the habit as its smallest complete version. “Open the file and write one sentence.” Scale up only once the small version is boring — your own Second Nature rule.
Cost — nothing.
5Write the goal down, specificallyB
From Think and Grow Rich and Covey — Hill’s one salvageable practice.
Locke and Latham’s goal-setting theory rests on hundreds of studies: specific, difficult goals beat vague or easy ones, given commitment and feedback. The “three percent of Yale students” study is a myth and never happened. A real experiment by Gail Matthews found people who wrote goals and sent weekly progress to a friend achieved substantially more.
Run it: specific, dated, written, and told to one person who will ask. Hill’s daily reading ritual is theatre; the writing and the accountability are the active ingredients.
Cost — ten minutes once, one message a week.
6Protect blocks of undivided attentionB
From Deep Work.
Task-switching carries a measurable penalty, in the range of twenty to forty percent in lab tasks, and attention residue means part of your mind stays on the previous task. What has not been shown is that four hours a day makes you the elite performer Newport promises.
Run it: one block, sixty to ninety minutes, phone in another room, one task. During term your two spare hours should be one such block, not two hours of fragments.
Cost — the discipline to close things, which is why it ranks below the tactics needing none.
7Warmth before competence, and use their nameB
From Carnegie — the base layer of your Charisma page.
Person-perception research, especially Fiske and Cuddy’s warmth-competence model, finds warmth is judged first and carries more weight in whether you are trusted. Genuine interest and remembered details raise liking study after study.
Run it: in a first exchange, ask one real question and use the name once in reply. With students, learn the names in week one.
Cost — attention.
8Loss framing to move yourselfA
From Thinking, Fast and Slow.
Loss aversion replicates at scale: losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please. The self-help application is commitment devices, and those have trial evidence — people who put money on the line, forfeited on failure, succeed at higher rates.
Run it: for a goal that keeps slipping, stake something. A sum you would hate to lose, held by a friend, returned on proof. Or a public deadline.
Cost — whatever you stake, and only if you fail.
9Reciprocity and small requests, deliberatelyA
From Influence.
Cialdini’s principles have field experiments behind them, including studies with millions of participants on giving and tax compliance. A small unrequested favour raises compliance with a later request substantially. This is the mechanism under most of Carnegie.
Run it: when you need something from a colleague, an admin or a parent, give first, cheaply and unasked. Then ask.
Cost — five minutes, and the willingness to be slightly strategic about kindness.
10Active listening, reflected backB
From Covey’s “seek first to understand”, and Carnegie.
Reflective listening is among the best-tested pieces of counselling practice, sitting inside motivational interviewing, which has meta-analytic support for changing health behaviours. People who feel heard rate the listener more highly and are more persuadable.
Run it: before you answer, say the other person’s point back in one sentence and ask if you have it right. With a defensive student or an anxious parent this changes the temperature of the room.
Cost — one sentence, and the patience not to jump in.
11Externalise the open loopsB
From Getting Things Done — the one piece worth keeping.
Unfinished tasks intrude on attention, the Zeigarnik effect, and a 2011 experiment found that simply writing a plan for the task — not doing it — stopped the intrusion. You do not need Allen’s apparatus of contexts and weekly reviews.
Run it: one list, on one device, and the rule that anything nagging gets written down with its next action before you return to what you were doing.
Cost — thirty seconds each time.
12Present-moment attention, ten minutesB
From The Power of Now, and Frankl by implication.
A 2014 meta-analysis in a major medical journal found mindfulness programs produce small-to-moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and pain, and no evidence for the grander claims. Tolle’s cosmology adds nothing to the practice.
Run it: ten minutes, attention on breath, return when it wanders. The Breathing Room already does this. The evidence is for regular practice over weeks, not one long session.
Cost — ten minutes a day, a real cost, which is why it sits mid-table.
13Self-compassion after failure, not self-criticismB
From Daring Greatly, by proximity.
Brown’s own work is qualitative, but Neff’s self-compassion research has meta-analyses showing moderate effects on wellbeing and, more usefully, that people who meet failure with self-compassion are more likely to try again than those who meet it with harshness. This contradicts the tough-guy framing in half the books on the list.
Run it: after a failure the sentence is “that went badly, and I will do the next one better” — not “I am hopeless”. Talk to yourself as you would to a student who tried.
Cost — a change of wording.
14Perseverance, not passionB
From Grit — the half that survives.
The meta-analysis that took grit apart found perseverance-of-effort still predicts academic performance after controlling for conscientiousness, while consistency-of-interest adds nothing. The usable finding is smaller than the book: keep going through difficulty, and forget the injunction to find one lifelong passion.
Run it: define persistence per task, not per life. Finish the thing in front of you. Your Mental Strength page already covers this.
Cost — nothing new.
15Pick the process goal, not the outcome goalB
From Atomic Habits — systems not goals — and Covey.
Goal-setting research finds process goals produce better performance and less anxiety when the task is complex or the outcome is partly out of your control. Clear overstates it — you still need the outcome to aim at — but the daily target should be the behaviour.
Run it: for anything longer than a month, track “did I do the behaviour”, not “am I closer”. The behaviour is under your control; the outcome is noisy.
Cost — nothing.
16Ask what the other person wants, instead of assumingC
From The 5 Love Languages — the only thing worth taking from it.
The five languages are not real categories, but the reflex the book installs — checking what your partner or child actually values rather than giving what you would want — is supported by the responsiveness literature. The evidence is correlational, hence the grade.
Run it: ask directly, once, what would make them feel cared for this week. Then do that.
Cost — one question.
17Batch the shallow workC
From The 4-Hour Workweek.
No study of batching as such; it inherits its support from the task-switching findings. Ferriss’s specific claim has one relevant trial, which found people limited to three email checks a day reported lower stress.
Run it: email and messages at two fixed times. Marking in one block, not dribbled across evenings.
Cost — tolerating the discomfort of an unchecked inbox.
18Question the base rate before decidingA C
From Thinking, Fast and Slow.
Base-rate neglect and the planning fallacy replicate reliably — that part is Grade A. The bad news is that debiasing training has mixed results; knowing about a bias does not reliably stop you committing it. The good news is that one move does improve estimates: asking how long similar projects took other people.
Run it: before committing to a timeline, a build or a money decision, ask what happened to the last five people who tried this. Your Shaping and Adapting outside judge is the same move.
Cost — one question.
19Meaning as a resource, not a moodB C
From Man’s Search for Meaning.
Meaning in life is one of the strongest correlates of wellbeing and resilience, and in older adults it predicts survival. Interventions to increase it have small effects in trials. Frankl’s practical point survives: you cannot choose your circumstances, you can choose what they are for.
Run it: for a hard stretch, write one sentence naming who it is for. Your Honest Stop asks “done or covered”; this is the companion question — for whom.
Cost — one sentence.
20Track itB
From Atomic Habits — and it is the whole logic of the Forge.
Self-monitoring is among the most consistently effective behaviour-change techniques in the health literature; a large taxonomy review found it one of the few that reliably improved outcomes across diets and exercise. It works even when the tracking is crude.
Run it: you already run the daily log and the boxing scorecard. The evidence says the act of recording matters more than the analysis.
Cost — already paid.
21Standing on the shoulders of conscientiousnessA C
From Grit, Mindset and Outliers together, by subtraction.
Strip those three down and the trait that actually predicts outcomes is conscientiousness, the most robust personality predictor of job performance and health across a century of data. The catch: adult personality moves slowly and there is no book-length trick for it.
Run it: borrow the behaviours instead of waiting for the trait. If-then plans, environment design and tracking are what a conscientious person does by default.
Cost — nothing extra. This is the summary of one through twenty.
22Practice on the hard part with feedbackB
From Outliers, corrected.
Deliberate practice, the real concept, is targeted work on the specific weakness with immediate feedback and full attention. The 2014 meta-analysis found it explains a real share of variance in music and games, much less in education and professions. Ten thousand hours is not a finding; the structure is the transferable part, not the volume.
Run it: name the sub-skill you are worst at, do that one on its own, get feedback the same day. For teaching that means recording one lesson segment and watching it, not teaching another hundred lessons.
Cost — the discomfort of watching yourself.
23Clear the surfaces you work atC
From Kondo.
A small correlational study found women describing their homes as cluttered had flatter cortisol curves, and a handful of experiments found tidier environments reduce decision fatigue and improve persistence. None of it is strong, and there is no trial of the method. But it is cheap and it overlaps with environment design.
Run it: the desk and the counter, not the whole house. The evidence, such as it is, concerns the surfaces you look at while working.
Cost — fifteen minutes a week.
24Say why, onceC
From Start With Why.
Sinek’s neuroscience is fiction, but the organisational literature does find a clearly stated purpose predicts engagement, and people comply more with a request that includes a reason, even a weak one — the photocopier study, which did replicate for small requests.
Run it: when you set a task for a class, an assignment, or a rule for your daughters, attach the reason in one sentence. Not a speech.
Cost — one sentence.
25A written asset-and-liability lensC
From Rich Dad Poor Dad — the salvage.
Nothing else in Kiyosaki survives and his leverage advice is dangerous. But asking whether a purchase puts money in your pocket or takes it out is a decent lens, and the household-finance literature is unambiguous that low-cost index funds, automated contributions and avoiding consumer debt beat everything the book recommends. Your Capital page has the real version.
Run it: before any purchase over a threshold you set, ask which side of the line it sits on.
Cost — one question, and it saves money rather than costing it.
What to leave in the books
Three things to actively refuse, because they are not merely useless but tested and harmful or false.
Positive visualisation of the outcome as already achieved. The whole of The Secret and most of Peale, Hill, Murphy and Schwartz. It lowers effort. If you catch yourself doing it, add the obstacle and the plan — you have just turned it into tactic two.
Willpower as a muscle you drain. Ego depletion did not survive a twenty-three-laboratory replication. Do not plan your day around conserving willpower. Plan it around not needing any, which is tactics one, three and four.
The primary love language, ten thousand hours, and NLP state changes. All three tested directly and failed. The books are still readable; the claims are dead.
Order of operations
If you did only five of the twenty-five, in this order, you would have most of the value of the entire genre.
1
If-then plans for the three behaviours that matter most this term.
2
One environment change per behaviour.
3
Mental contrasting on the one goal you keep daydreaming about — and be ready for it to tell you no.
4
One protected block a week during term: the whole of your spare two hours.
5
Self-compassion as the default response to a bad day, because it is what keeps the first four running after the first failure.
Everything else is refinement. Most of it is already on protocols.html.

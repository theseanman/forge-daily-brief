Bias

Thirty biases graded on the evidence, the ten that actually cost you something, and the famous ones that did not survive replication. Recognition at the decision point — not a list to admire.

What actually works
Knowing helps a little. Practice helps more.B
The pessimistic line — that knowing about a bias does nothing to stop it — is overstated. Morewedge and colleagues gave people one training session against six biases. A single instructional video cut bias commission by at least 19%; a single interactive game with feedback cut it by at least 32%. Both were still holding two months later, and the effect generalised to biases the training had not covered.
But generic warnings fail. In a study of selection neglect, subjects who were explicitly told that the sample they were seeing had been filtered still reasoned as though it hadn’t. Only those given the actual filtering rule corrected. That is the design principle for this whole page: “beware survivorship bias” is demonstrated not to work. Naming the mechanism is what works.
So the page is built around triggers, not definitions. A definition you read once. A trigger is something you can feel happening — and the counter is a move you make in that moment, not an attitude you hold.
Most biases are cheap to be wrong about. Friction has a price and you have about two hours a week to spare in term time. The cost gate near the bottom of this page says which decisions are worth the checking and which you should simply make and move on from. A protocol that makes you audit every judgement has cost you more than the biases would have.
The ten that cost you
Ranked by evidence strength × what it costs when you’re wrong × how often it hits the things you actually do.
1OverprecisionA
Your confidence intervals are far too narrow. Not that you’re wrong about the middle — that you’re wrong about the width.
Feels like being able to name a single number without effort. “About three weeks.” “Maybe forty students.” The ease is the warning.
Give a range before a number — and when you’ve given one, widen it. Ask 90% intervals of people and the truth lands inside roughly 30–60% of the time.
Ask what would put it outside — name one concrete way it could come in far above your high end. Considering alternatives is the countermeasure with the best support.
Decompose — break the estimate into parts you can each bound, then recombine. This works better than trying to be humble about the whole.
Income “The course will take a summer” is a point estimate wearing a duration costume. What’s the version where it takes two?
Stock system A forward-test edge quoted without an interval is not a finding, it’s a hope with a decimal point.
Room 148 “They’ll need one class for this” — give it a range, then plan the filler for the short end.
Of the three overconfidences, this is the one that holds. Overestimating your performance and overplacing yourself against others both reverse on easy tasks — people routinely underrate themselves there. Overprecision has few if any documented reversals.
2The planning fallacyA
You predict this project from the inside — the steps you can see — and the inside view has no room for what usually goes wrong.
Feels like visualising the sequence and it running clean. Also: a deadline that assumes nothing else lands that week.
Use the last five, not the plan — how long did the last five comparable things actually take? That number, not your reasoning about this one.
Recalling isn’t enough — in the original studies, merely remembering past overruns didn’t help. Only explicitly linking past cases to this prediction did, and it moved on-time completion from 29% to 60%.
Predict the interruptions separately — marking, a sick kid, a PLT day. They are not noise; they are the reason the inside view is short.
Builds Every protocol page that was “an evening’s work.” You have a five-case reference class sitting in your own history.
Room 148 Unit packages: count from the last completed unit, not from the outline of the next one.
Home “I’ll be done in twenty minutes” said to a fourteen-year-old is a promise, and it is usually the inside view talking.
Honest caveat: the fix is advocated harder than the evidence supports. Reference-class forecasting has encouraging but inconsistent results in the field, with named failures — and no meta-analysis of its accuracy. Use it because the inside view is demonstrably short, not because the remedy is proven.
3Selection neglectB
You reason from the sample in front of you as though it arrived by chance. It almost never did.
Feels like a pattern emerging from examples. Especially examples someone else assembled — a list of winners, a set of testimonials, the strategies that made it into a book.
Ask what got filtered out — not “is this biased?” but “what would a case that didn’t make it into this sample look like, and where would I find one?”
Name the filter out loud — the experimental finding is specific: a general warning that selection exists does not reduce the error. Stating the actual mechanism does.
Watch confidence rise with sample size — in a filtered sample, more data makes you more certain and no more correct. That feeling of “now I’ve seen enough” is the trap closing.
Stock system Backtests survive because they were kept. The filter is you, choosing which to continue.
Income Every course that sold well is visible. The ones that didn’t are not on anyone’s shelf.
Room 148 The strategy that worked for the class you remember. You remember it because it worked.
4Myside biasB
You search, weigh and remember evidence in the direction of what you already hold. Not stupidity — the effect is essentially unrelated to intelligence.
Feels like reading something that agrees with you and feeling informed. Also: the search you run being phrased as the answer you want.
Consider the opposite, concretely — not “could I be wrong” but “write the strongest version of the case against, as someone who believes it would put it.”
Set the disconfirmer in advance — what result would make you drop this? Named before you look, or it moves when you do.
Check the motive — the bias roughly triples when you’re defending something you’re committed to, and shrinks when the uncongenial information is actually useful to you. Notice which mode you’re in.
Stock system “Is the edge real?” searched by someone who has built it for a year is not a neutral query.
Forge OS “Is this protocol working?” asked of a page you wrote. The honest version has a named failure condition.
Room 148 Evidence that a method is working, collected by the person who chose the method.
The selective-exposure effect averages d = 0.38 — real but moderate. The moderators are bigger than the main effect, which is why the practical move is to check your motive rather than to assume a constant level of distortion.
5Outcome biasA
You judge the quality of a decision by how it turned out, when the only information available at the time is what should count.
Feels like reviewing something that went badly and finding the mistake obvious. Also: a win making the process feel validated.
Judge the decision on what was knowable then — write it down before the result arrives, or you will not be able to reconstruct it afterwards.
Separate the two verdicts — “bad outcome” and “bad decision” are different findings. Say which one you mean.
Don’t trust your own exemption — in the replication, people who explicitly said outcomes shouldn’t matter still showed the effect at d = 0.64. Knowing the rule does not buy you out of it.
Stock system A losing trade taken on a correct signal is a correct trade. Changing the rule after it is how the system dies.
Fantasy Starting the right player who had a bad day is not an error to fix.
Room 148 A lesson that fell flat with one class. Sometimes the plan was wrong; sometimes it was a Friday.
The replication found the effect larger than the original — d = 0.77 to 1.10. It shows up in blame and punishment but not, interestingly, in judgements of whether an act was ethical.
6Hindsight biasB
Once you know the outcome, your memory of what you expected quietly moves toward it. It is what makes outcome bias feel justified.
Feels like “I knew that would happen” — said with genuine sincerity, which is the problem.
Write the prediction down — the only real defence. Memory cannot be audited; a file can.
Include your confidence — “I thought it would work” drifts. “I gave it 70%” does not.
Extend the courtesy outward — someone whose call went wrong may have made the right call. You will not feel that they did.
Stock system Post-mortems written from memory are fiction with a spreadsheet attached.
Builds “I always thought that approach was shaky” — check whether you said so at the time.
Meta-analytic r = .17 — smaller than the textbook impression, and the almanac-question lab task inflates it. Direct replications of the classic studies land at d = 0.40–0.60. Real, moderate, and cheaply defeated by writing things down. Shaping and Adapting already carries the prediction-writing discipline.
7Base-rate neglectA
Given a vivid specific case and a boring background rate, you weight the case and drop the rate.
Feels like a description that fits. The better the fit, the more completely the base rate disappears.
Ask “out of how many?” — before asking how likely this one is. The denominator first, then the case.
Convert to counts — “8 out of 1,000” rather than “0.8%”. Natural frequencies raise correct solutions from about 4% to about 24%: a real gain, and still three quarters getting it wrong.
The rate is the starting point, not a tiebreaker — you update from it. Most people treat it as a footnote to the story.
Stock system A signal with a great hit rate on a rare setup. How often does the setup occur?
Health A screening result means nothing without the prevalence of the thing screened for.
Income “This person built exactly this and it worked” — out of how many who tried?
Worth knowing: the population is bimodal, not uniformly bad. Roughly half of people reason correctly when the format is right. The heuristic account of why people fail is itself in some trouble — modelling finds little support for it.
8Sunk costB
What you have already spent pulls on a decision that should only be about what happens next.
Feels like “after all this work.” Also: reluctance to look at a project directly.
Ask the fresh-start question — knowing what I know now, and starting from zero, would I begin this today?
Decide the kill condition when you start — written in advance, it is a rule. Written afterwards, it is a negotiation you will win.
Separate the loss from the decision — the time is gone either way. Continuing does not recover it; it only adds to it.
Triage The quarterly live/paused/dead register exists because this bias is strongest on the projects you are proudest of.
Builds Three weeks into an approach that isn’t working. The three weeks are not an argument.
Pooled lab effect ES ≈ 0.50, and it survives with real money. But the field evidence is weaker — one large field experiment found no effect at all, while the online version of the same study did. Robust in controlled settings; real-world size uncertain.
9AnchoringA
The first number on the table moves your estimate, even when you know where it came from and know it’s arbitrary.
Feels like adjusting from a figure someone else said. The adjustment always feels sufficient. It isn’t.
Estimate before you look — your number first, written down, then the anchor. Reversing the order is the whole defence.
Name the anchor when you spot one — a listed price, an opening offer, last year’s figure, someone else’s guess.
Don’t rely on expertise — 600-plus real trial judges were reliably moved by numeric anchors on sentences and damages.
Income Any pricing conversation. Have your number before the call.
Marking The first essay in the pile sets the scale for the rest of it.
Builds “Should take about two days” said by anyone, including you, an hour ago.
Classic numeric anchoring replicated at d = 1.27 to 2.60 in Many Labs 1 — among the largest effects in the whole project. But incidental anchoring, where a stray number in the environment supposedly shifts you, failed outright (d = 0.04). The version where a jersey number changes your estimate is not supported.
10The bias blind spotA
You can see all nine of the above operating in other people. From the inside, your own judgement just feels like looking at the facts.
Feels like having read this page and thought mainly of other people while doing it.
Treat introspection as the wrong instrument — you cannot feel a bias working. You can only check the output against something external.
Outsource the check — one named person who will say it plainly. This is the standing move in Shaping and Adapting: who, other than me, says this is working?
Use the record, not the feeling — written predictions, actual completion times, the trade log. Instruments, not impressions.
All of it The reason this protocol’s Drill tab is recognition-based: you are training to spot the shape in a situation, not to hold an opinion about yourself.
Replicates larger than the original: mini-meta d = −1.00 across three studies, plus an independent preregistered replication. One of the better-supported findings on the page — which is inconvenient, because it is the one you will most want to except yourself from.
The catalogue — thirty, graded
One line each: what it is, and where it bites you. Grade is evidence strength, not importance. The ten above are marked ★.
Estimating and forecasting
★ Overprecision (the ease of naming one number) A
Intervals far too narrow. 90% intervals capture the truth 30–60% of the time. The most robust item here.
★ Planning fallacy (the clean sequence) A
Inside-view time estimates run short. Only 30% finished by their own predicted date. Not universal — short tasks are often overestimated.
★ Anchoring (adjusting from someone’s number) A
Huge for explicit numeric anchors, including among judges. Incidental environmental anchors did not replicate.
★ Base-rate neglect (a description that fits) A
Specific case beats background rate. Frequencies help a lot in relative terms and leave most people still wrong.
Present bias (now is worth more) B
Real, and stronger over effort and consumption (β ≈ 0.66) than over money (β ≈ 0.82) — the money experiments most textbooks use are the weakest measurement.
Duration neglect (how long it went doesn’t register) B
Retrospective judgement of a bounded episode tracks the peak and the end, not the length. Holds for discrete episodes; fails for whole days and holidays.
Evidence and judgement
★ Myside bias (agreement feels like information) B
d = 0.38 for selective exposure, roughly tripled when defending a commitment. Essentially unrelated to intelligence.
★ Selection neglect (a pattern in a curated set) B
Survivorship, in its experimentally tested form. Over-inference of ~8–10 points; a general warning doesn’t fix it, naming the filter does.
★ Outcome bias (it went badly, so it was wrong) A
d = 0.77–1.10 on replication, and d = 0.64 even among people who said outcomes shouldn’t count.
★ Hindsight bias (“I knew it”) B
Memory of your own prediction drifts toward the result. Meta-analytic r = .17; direct replications d = 0.40–0.60.
Availability (what comes to mind first) B
Judging frequency by recalled instances holds up and beats the affect account for frequency judgements. The “ease of retrieval” version is a different claim — see the ledger.
Law of small numbers (a run that means something) B
Expecting short samples to look like the population. Three good weeks of forward-test results is a short sample.
Regression to the mean, misread (the intervention that “worked”) A
Extreme results drift back toward average on their own. Whatever you did after the worst week gets the credit.
Illusory pattern detection (structure in noise) C
Individual differences in seeing patterns are real and measurable. The famous claim that losing control causes it did not replicate — see the ledger.
Sticking and quitting
★ Sunk cost (“after all this work”) B
ES ≈ 0.50 in the lab and it survives incentives. Weaker and sometimes absent in the field.
Status quo / default (the option already selected) A
d = 0.68 on take-up, ~27 percentage points. But the downstream effects are far smaller than advertised — see the ledger.
Endowment effect (yours is worth more) A
WTA/WTP ≈ 2.5×, and it survived the procedural critique that was supposed to dissolve it. Vanishes with real market experience.
Loss aversion (losses loom larger) C
λ ≈ 1.96 as a literature average. Genuinely contested whether it’s a preference parameter at all — one 2025 reanalysis gets λ ≈ 1.07 on symmetric, unordered presentations. Don’t build on the 2× figure.
Reading people
★ Bias blind spot (this page is about other people) A
Mini-meta d = −1.00, larger than the original. Replicates cross-culturally.
Correspondence bias (they are like that) A
Replicated at d = 1.82 in Many Labs 2. But whether it’s an error is under live challenge — the pattern falls out of correct Bayesian inference once you drop the assumption that situations fully determine behaviour.
Halo effect (good at one thing, good full stop) B
Robust, but trait-specific: strong on dimensions near the cue, weak on integrity and concern for others. Attractive targets are also judged more accurately, which complicates the pure-bias reading.
Curse of knowledge (this is obvious) B
Real but about half the advertised size (d = 0.20 in a seven-experiment, N = 3,074 estimate). Still the central hazard of explaining anything to a beginner.
Illusion of transparency (they can tell) D
Widely taught; I could find no high-powered preregistered replication. Graded D for absence of evidence, not for evidence of absence.
Self-assessment error (the Dunning-Kruger picture) C
Low performers are less accurate self-assessors. They are not more confident in absolute terms — that part is wrong. See the ledger.
Framing and groups
Framing (200 saved vs 400 die) A
d = 0.62 in Many Labs 1, significant in 86% of 36 samples. Shrinks sharply when the complementary outcome is stated explicitly.
Group polarisation (the meeting ends further out) B
Discussion among like-minded people moves the group toward the extreme, not the middle. Robust and replicated. Not the same thing as groupthink, which isn’t — see the ledger.
Peak-end rule (how it ended is how it was) B
Strong for discrete, bounded episodes (Bayes factors >10,000 in odour sequences). Fails for multi-episode stretches — whole days track duration-weighted averages instead.
Affect heuristic (risky, therefore useless) B
Inverse risk-benefit correlation replicates at r ≈ −.85. But the manipulation only works one way: raising risk lowers perceived benefit, and lowering benefit does not raise perceived risk.
Illusion of control (choosing makes it likelier) C
Skill-cue versions hold better than choice versions. The textbook lottery-ticket mechanism failed across 17 preregistered experiments, N = 10,825.
Optimism bias (it won’t happen to me) D
The standard comparative paradigm generates the result from unbiased agents via scale artifacts. Don’t cite the driver statistic. See the ledger.
The ledger — taught, but not supported
Every one of these is in wide circulation and several are in books on your shelf. A page about clear thinking that repeats dead findings has refuted itself.
Ego depletion DEAD
Willpower as a depletable fuel. A 23-lab replication (N = 2,141) got d = 0.04; a 36-lab paradigmatic test (N = 3,531) got d = 0.06 with Bayes factor 4:1 favouring the null. One 12-lab study using a stronger manipulation found d = 0.10–0.16, so something small may survive — but the glucose account has no support at all. Plan days that need no willpower rather than budgeting it.
Social / behavioural priming DEAD
Elderly words making you walk slower; “professor” priming making you smarter. The registered replication of professor priming across 40 labs found 0.14% against an original 13%. Not the same as semantic priming, which is among the most replicable effects in cognitive psychology — the two get conflated constantly.
Groupthink DEAD
Janis’s model is retrospective case narrative, not a tested theory. After 30+ years the evidence has failed to support its central predictions; one review found only nine independent studies of the core cohesion link. The symptoms show up in ordinary groups lacking every specified antecedent, which empties the model. Group polarisation is the real, replicated phenomenon — use that instead.
The hot hand fallacy REVERSED
The most interesting one. Miller and Sanjurjo showed the original analysis contained a streak-selection bias: in a finite sequence, the hit rate after three makes is a downward-biased estimator, by about 8 percentage points in the original design. Correcting it turns a null (+3pp) into +13pp, p < .01. Their line: the belief is not a cognitive illusion, and the “fallacy” can itself be viewed as a fallacy. Note carefully what this does and does not license — streakiness being real does not mean your sense of it is correctly sized.
Control threat → seeing patterns FAILED
The Science finding that feeling out of control makes people see illusory patterns. Seven experiments (N ≈ 771) found no effect on any relevant measure, with Bayesian analysis supporting the null. The individual-difference version survives: people high in paranormal belief do show elevated false-alarm rates. Correlation real, causal induction not.
Choice → illusion of control FAILED
Langer’s lottery-ticket study is still the standard illustration. Seventeen preregistered experiments, N = 10,825: choosing rarely made people feel more likely to win unless choosing actually improved the odds. Where effects appeared they traced to pre-existing wrong beliefs about the options.
Ease of retrieval CONTESTED
The claim that the difficulty of recalling examples is itself informational — recall twelve assertive acts and feel less assertive than recalling six. The meta-analysis shows substantial publication bias, and two large well-powered tests (N = 1,325 and a preregistered N = 659) both found nothing.
Comparative optimism CONTESTED
“Most drivers think they’re above average, therefore self-enhancement.” The inference is invalid: with a skewed distribution most drivers are above the mean, and scale attenuation plus base-rate regression generate the pattern from unbiased agents. The same work found a severity effect — negative outcomes overestimated, the opposite of optimism.
Dunning-Kruger, as usually told CONTESTED
The pattern is real; the story is wrong. Low performers overestimate and high performers underestimate, which is largely regression to the mean plus a better-than-average effect. One analysis (N = 929) found the relation essentially linear and called it mostly a statistical artefact — though that critique has itself been attacked on coding grounds, and a design avoiding the double-dipping problem found the effect survives. Defensible: low performers self-assess less accurately. Not defensible: that they are more confident than high performers. They are not.
Defaults change outcomes OVERSOLD
Not a bias but the most-repeated application of one. Five countries switching to opt-out organ donation: donation odds changed −1.67%, with the Bayes factor running against the policy as a predictor. Auto-enrolment raises retirement savings by 0.6% of income, not the naive 2.6% — a 77% markdown once turnover and leakage are counted. The default reliably moves the registration. It does not reliably move the outcome.
The pattern worth carrying out of the ledger. The survivors were tested with large multi-lab or randomised designs on the original paradigm: framing, anchoring, endowment, outcome bias, overprecision, the blind spot. The casualties were mostly single-study social-psychology effects with a mechanism story attached. And repeatedly — the phenomenon survives while the mechanism does not. That is worth knowing before you believe the next striking finding.
The cost gate
Checking is not free. Run the friction where being wrong is expensive, and nowhere else.
Spend the friction when the decision is hard to reverse — money committed, a course direction, a rule change in the stock system, something said to one of your daughters.
Spend it when you’ll be held to a number — any estimate someone else will plan around.
Spend it when you notice you’re defending — myside bias roughly triples there, which is exactly where the check pays.
Skip it on anything cheap, fast or reversible — which is most of a day. A wrong call you can undo in five minutes does not deserve a checklist.
Skip it mid-performance — attending to the steps of a practised skill degrades it. Not while you are teaching, not during an hour with the girls.
The gate in one line: if you could not undo it by Friday, check it. Otherwise decide and move.

The one thing
Where did this sample come from?
Asked of a set of examples, a run of results, a memory, a case that fits. It is the single question that covers selection, availability, base rates and small samples at once — and unlike “am I being biased?”, it has an answer you can go and find.
Failure modes
Bias as a debating move — naming someone’s bias is not an argument against their claim. It is the cheapest possible rebuttal and it ends inquiry rather than advancing it.
The catalogue as an identity — knowing thirty biases makes you feel like a clear thinker. The blind-spot evidence says that feeling is uncorrelated with being one.
Running it live — auditing your own reasoning mid-lesson or mid-conversation costs more than the bias would have. This belongs at decision points.
Checking everything — the friction budget is real and small. The cost gate exists because a protocol that makes you audit every judgement has already lost you more time than it saves.
Confidence in the grades — these are current as of writing. Several items on this page moved in the last decade, and some will move again. A grade is a snapshot, not a fact.
This page as the work — reading about clear thinking feels like doing it. The only test is whether one decision this week goes differently.
Evidence
A Strong. Large multi-lab, meta-analytic or preregistered support on the original paradigm. Anchoring (Many Labs 1; Rachlinski, Wistrich & Guthrie 2015 on judges), framing (Many Labs 1, d = 0.62), outcome bias (Feldman et al. 2023), bias blind spot (Chandrashekar et al. 2021), endowment (Fehr, Hakimov & Kübler 2015), overprecision (Moore, Tenney & Haran), planning fallacy (Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994), base-rate format effects (McDowell & Jacobs 2017), default take-up (Jachimowicz et al. 2019), correspondence bias (Many Labs 2, d = 1.82).

B Good, with variance or boundary conditions. Myside bias (Hart et al. 2009, d = 0.38), selection neglect (López-Pérez, Pintér & Sánchez-Mangas 2022), hindsight (Christensen-Szalanski & Willham 1991, r = .17; Chen & Feldman 2021 replications), sunk cost (Roth, Robbert & Straus 2015), peak-end (Scheibehenne & Coppin 2019, and the day-level failures), curse of knowledge (Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt 2014, d = 0.20), present bias (Cheung, Tymula & Wang), affect heuristic (Efendić et al. 2022), availability by recall (Pachur, Hertwig & Steinmann 2012), debiasing training (Morewedge et al. 2015; Sellier, Scopelliti & Morewedge on field transfer).

C Contested or mechanism unresolved. Loss aversion (Brown et al. 2024 vs Yechiam & Zeif 2025), illusion of control, self-assessment error, illusory pattern detection, action bias, zero-risk bias. Real phenomena with disputed causes or disputed sizes. Don’t build a decision rule on these.

D Thin or artifactual. Illusion of transparency (no adequate modern replication found), optimism bias (comparative paradigm confounded). Listed because they are widely believed, not because they are established.

X Ledger. Failed, reversed, or oversold. Detailed above with the specific study that settled it.

The ranking — evidence × cost-when-wrong × frequency in your actual week — is this page’s own reasoning. The grades are from the literature; the order is a judgement call.

Verification status. Every grade and figure on this page was checked against primary sources or meta-analyses at build time, not quoted from memory. Three specific items were flagged as second-hand and should be re-checked before being quoted anywhere public: the ease-of-retrieval replication test statistics, the Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen planning-fallacy caveat, and Eagly et al.’s halo effect sizes.

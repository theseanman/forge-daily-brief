The Getting-Paid Protocol

Distribution over production. The bottleneck isn't what you make — it's getting it in front of people and charging for it.

Read this first You don't have a talent problem — you have a permission problem. You're sitting on an arsenal you built and refuse to sell, and you walk back through that door every September because of it. You're not far away. You're one skill away — not one more product — and you learn hard skills for breakfast. Selling is smaller than coding, smaller than running your own body like an experiment. Drop the story that it's beyond you. It's the only thing in the way.
The six pillars
1The bottleneck is distribution
Replacing a salary is a small-numbers problem. Seeing the real number kills the vagueness that keeps you building instead of selling.
Run the math — exit number ÷ course price ÷ 30 = your daily target. It's almost always shockingly small.
Price beats volume — doubling price halves the humans you must find, and finding humans is the hard part.
ReasoningThe salary doesn't care how many things you've made — only how many people paid you.
2One offer, one audience
Sprawl is the tax you already pay. Concentration is the cure — the same discipline as your project triage.
Name the one person — specific enough they recognize themselves and someone else feels excluded.
Park the rest — whatever doesn't serve that one offer, shelve it the way you triage projects.
VerifiedNarrow positioning out-converts broad. Relevance beats reach at your scale.
3Publish on a cadence
The audience is built by showing up on a rhythm, not by occasional brilliance. Consistency is the engine; intensity is a spark that fades.
One channel, sustainable rhythm — a cadence you can hit in your worst teaching week. Weekly is plenty.
Every piece does one job — prove you understand the reader's problem better than they do.
VerifiedMere-exposure: familiarity alone builds liking and trust. A broken cadence teaches people to stop expecting you.
4An offer people pay for
A product is what you built. An offer is problem, promise, proof, price — arranged so a specific person feels the yes.
Write the four paragraphs — problem, promise, proof, price — before you touch a landing page.
Price is a message — set it against a real anchor; a three-tier menu lifts the middle.
VerifiedAnchoring, three-tier choice, loss aversion are real. Use the mechanism, keep the truth: real anchor yes, fake "was" price no.
5Selling without the ick
The ick is manipulation, not selling. Honest persuasion is just helping the right person make a decision that's good for them.
Value first, small yeses — give real value, then ask a free read, an email, a low-risk offer, the course.
Tell the whole truth — including who it's NOT for. Disqualifying the wrong buyer builds trust.
ReasoningYou're bad at hype — and a skeptical, evidence-first audience trusts exactly that. Your honesty is the edge, not the handicap.
6The flywheel
You already live by measurement. Point it at the funnel, steer by the leading signals, let the lagging ones lag.
Four numbers, not forty — new audience, subscribers, sales, revenue.
Fix one link — each month, attack only the biggest drop-off.
LoreIgnore quoted benchmarks ("1–2% of a list buys"). Replace them fast with your own N=1 data — the only sample that predicts you.
The one thing
Stop treating "I made it" as the finish line. The finish line is "a stranger paid for it." You're already extraordinary at the first half. This is the year you get deliberate about the second.
The standard
People with a fraction of your ability are already getting paid for lesser work — not because they're better, but because they were willing to do the one thing you won't: sell. Let that gnaw. Not envy — a standard. You close gaps like this; it's how you're built. When something you couldn't do sits there being done by someone lesser, you learn to do it — and once it's closed, it stays closed. The flinch away from putting your work in front of people and asking to be paid is just another thing trying to rule you. You don't stay ruled; you've proven that more than once. A man who can build anything but won't be paid for it is letting the discomfort win. So don't. Decide it's unacceptable that lesser people did what you haven't — and go take it.
The clock
Every September back through that door is a specific year with your family, traded away — and you don't get it back. That's not guilt, it's the clearest math in here: the clock runs whether you sell or not. You already do hard reps regardless of mood — sleep, protocols, the drill. Point one tenth of that at selling and you win, because almost no one who can build like you will also sell. That overlap is rare, and it's yours.
When you don't feel like it
Motivation is weather. On the flat days, do the smallest next rep — one post, one email, one line of the offer. The identity isn't "feels motivated to sell"; it's "ships the next rep regardless." And the asymmetry is absurdly in your favour: worst case of trying is a few awkward posts; worst case of not trying is the exact life you're leaving, on repeat.
The line in the sand You've built the hard thing a hundred times. Choosing not to sell it is the only thing between you and the door out. So choose again: one offer, one audience, first piece this week, the door to the course at the bottom of it — then again next week, whether you feel like it or not, until the small number of people who need to say yes have said it. You don't need to become someone else. Let the builder you already are point one honest ounce of force at getting paid. Now go.

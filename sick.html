The Sick Day Protocol

How respiratory bugs actually reach you, what measurably keeps them out, and what to do in the first day — the only window where most of the useful moves still work. Every claim carries a grade, and every piece of folklore gets a verdict. General information, not medical advice: a pharmacist or HealthLink BC (8-1-1) outranks this page.

How to read the grades
AConsistent randomised trials, a solid meta-analysis, or settled public-health fact. BReal trials with real limits — small, unblinded, mixed — or strong observational data. CA single trial, a lab or mechanism finding, or evidence that points both ways. DNo human evidence. Tradition, anecdote, or expert habit.
Folklore also gets a verdict: SUPPORTED PARTLY TRUE MYTH HARMFUL UNPROVEN. A grade says how strong the evidence is; a verdict says what it concludes.
How you catch itNobody catches a cold from the weather. A virus gets in through a door, at a time when the guard is thin. Both halves matter.
How it travelsB
Three routes, and their share differs by virus. The room, the hands, and the face are the whole map.
Shared air — small particles that hang in a room for minutes to hours. The dominant route for flu and COVID, and the reason crowded, stuffy rooms are where outbreaks happen.
Close range — larger droplets from a cough, sneeze or conversation within a metre or two. Face-to-face at a desk counts.
Hands to face — especially important for rhinovirus, the main common-cold virus. It survives for hours on hands and surfaces and gets in through the nose and the eyes, which drain into the nose. The mouth is a minor door.
For a teacher this means handed-in paper, shared markers and door handles are carriers, and the classroom air is the bigger exposure in flu season.
The clockA
You are usually infectious before you feel anything, and so was whoever gave it to you.
Incubation — common cold roughly 1–3 days; flu 1–4 days, typically about 2; COVID now about 3; RSV 4–6.
Most contagious — colds in the first two to three days of symptoms; flu from the day before symptoms to about a week after.
What that means — the exposure that made you sick was probably two or three days ago, from someone who felt fine. The useful question is not “who gave me this” but “who am I giving it to today”.
What thins the guardB
Exposure is constant. Whether it becomes an illness depends heavily on the state you are in when it arrives.
Short sleep — in the best-known study (Prather, 2015), 164 adults wore sleep trackers for a week, then had a cold virus dripped into their noses in quarantine. Those averaging under six hours were about four times as likely to get sick as those sleeping over seven. Six to seven hours showed no significant difference. Sleep outranked age, stress, income and smoking as a predictor.
Chronic stress — Cohen’s virus-challenge studies (1991 onward) found a dose-response: the more psychological stress people reported, the more often a deliberate exposure turned into a cold.
A cold nose — a 2022 study (Huang, Bleier and colleagues) found the inside of the nose drops about 5°C in cold air, and at that temperature nasal cells released roughly 42% fewer of the virus-trapping vesicles that form part of the nose’s first defence. That result is from cells in a dish, so treat it as mechanism, not proof.
Load — young children average up to 10–12 colds a year. Standing in a room of teenagers every day is a higher dose than most jobs.
Sleep is graded as association, not cause: the authors themselves said it is still open whether sleeping more protects you. It is the strongest predictor on the list, and it is also the one you control.
Why SeptemberB
Every year, colds spike within weeks of school starting — before the weather turns.
The back-to-school surge — rhinovirus rises sharply in September as children re-mix after summer, and it shows up in hospital data as a reliable autumn peak in asthma flare-ups.
Your version — a new building, new students, new germs your immune system has not met, and the extra load of a new job hitting sleep and stress at the same moment. Expect the first cold of the year soon. It is not a failure of the protocol; it is the base rate.

Prevention, rankedOrdered by what you get for the effort. The top of the list is boring on purpose.
1Seven hours, protectedB
The biggest single lever in the evidence, and it costs nothing but an evening.
Guard the floor, not the average — the risk in the Prather data sat below six hours. A string of short nights in a stressful week is exactly the setup.
Front-load it in exposure weeks — the week school starts, a week after a sick room, the week before a trip.
The whole playbook lives in the Sleep protocol. The brief already tracks it; a run of low scores in a heavy week is the signal to act.
2VaccinesA
The only item here that reliably prevents severe flu and COVID rather than shaving days off a cold.
Flu, every autumn — effectiveness varies by season; in years when the vaccine matches the circulating strains it cuts the risk of illness needing medical care by roughly 40–60%, and it lowers the odds of a severe case even in poorly matched years. It is reformulated each year, which is why last year’s does not carry over.
COVID, on current guidance — eligibility and timing change year to year. Check ImmunizeBC for this fall’s program before assuming.
It cannot give you the flu — the injected vaccine contains no live virus. A sore arm or a day of feeling off is the immune response, not an infection.
3Hands off the faceB
Across trials in schools, homes and offices, hand hygiene cut respiratory illness by about 14% (Cochrane, 2023, moderate certainty). It did not clearly reduce lab-confirmed flu, which fits flu being mostly airborne.
Wash at the transitions — after collecting papers or handling shared materials, before eating, when you get home.
Sanitiser for the gaps — alcohol rub is a reasonable stand-in when a sink is not near.
Eyes and nose — those are the doors for rhinovirus. The habit of rubbing your eyes at the end of a marking stack is the one to catch.
4Air that movesC
Airborne viruses build up in still, crowded rooms. Diluting the air lowers the dose everyone is breathing.
Open what opens — a window cracked, a door propped, especially in the last minutes of a full class.
A CO₂ monitor as a gauge — CO₂ tracks exhaled air. Readings climbing well past 1,000 ppm mean the room is recycling a lot of breath; under about 800 is commonly used as a good-ventilation mark. It measures air change, not virus.
Portable HEPA filter — a reasonable add in a room with poor airflow.
Graded C because the case rests mostly on physics and observational school studies rather than clean trials. The mechanism is not in doubt; the size of the effect is.
5Move most daysB
In a prospective study of about 1,000 adults (Nieman, 2011), those doing aerobic exercise five or more days a week had roughly 40% fewer days with respiratory illness than the least active.
Regular and moderate — that is the pattern associated with fewer sick days. Observational, so fitter people may differ in other ways too.
Extreme bouts are different — marathon-type efforts are followed by more colds in several studies. Relevant around a big event, not a normal training week.
Smaller wallsC
Cheap, low-risk, weaker evidence. Worth knowing; not worth building a routine around unless you want to.
Gargle plain water — a Japanese trial (Satomura, 2005) had healthy adults gargle water three times a day and found about 36% fewer respiratory infections. One trial, unblinded.
Probiotics — a 2022 Cochrane review found fewer upper-respiratory infections with probiotics, at low-to-moderate certainty, with strains and doses all over the map. Details in the ledger.
Keep the nose warm — a scarf over the nose in cold wind follows the 2022 nasal-temperature finding. Mechanism only, costs nothing.
Masks — the 2023 Cochrane review found community mask trials uncertain, and no clear difference between N95s and surgical masks for lab-confirmed flu in the trials it pooled. Trials of this kind are hard to run, and a well-fitted respirator in a packed room during a bad season is a reasonable personal choice. The evidence does not settle it either way.

The first 48 hoursThe scratchy throat is a deadline, not a maybe. Most of what shortens an illness only works if it starts early, and waiting to see is the most common way to lose the window.
1The first tickleB
Hour zero. Act the same day, not tomorrow.
Zinc lozenges, dissolved slowly — zinc acetate or gluconate, adding up to more than 75 mg of elemental zinc across the day, one every two to three waking hours, started within 24 hours. Stop after about five days, or sooner if nausea starts.
Salt water, both ends — start a saline nasal rinse and a salt-water gargle today. How and why in the Relief section.
Cancel the hard session — a hard workout on day one is spending the resource you need.
Bed early tonight — the extra hour is the cheapest thing on this page.
The zinc evidence is genuinely disputed and that is worth knowing. Hemilä’s pooled analysis of lozenge trials at that dose found colds about a third shorter. The 2024 Cochrane review, which mixed lozenge types, doses and zinc used for prevention, rated it low certainty with colds perhaps two days shorter. Both agree the effect, if real, depends on dose and form — low-dose lozenges showed nothing.
2Is this a cold, or more?A
Hours 6 to 24. The answer decides whether there is a treatment window to catch.
Cold pattern — creeps in over a day or two, sore throat first, runny nose, little or no fever, you feel rough but functional.
Flu pattern — comes on in hours: fever, chills, aching muscles, a flattened feeling. That speed is the tell.
Test — a rapid COVID test at home. Keep a few in the cupboard before the season, not after.
Know the windows — flu antivirals such as oseltamivir work best started within about 48 hours of symptoms; COVID’s Paxlovid must start within five days. Both are mainly for people at higher risk of complications, and Paxlovid interacts with many medications. If you might qualify, call a pharmacist or 8-1-1 on day one, not day four.
3Teach or stay homeB
The current public-health rule, used across respiratory viruses: stay home until you have been fever-free for 24 hours without fever-reducing medication and your symptoms are improving overall.
Fever means home — not negotiable, and masking a fever with ibuprofen to get through a teaching day does not change it.
A mild cold without fever — working is reasonable. Keep your distance from students’ desks, wash hands after coughing, and consider a mask for a few days to protect the room.
Decide the night before — morning decisions made feeling half-awake default to going in.
Build the sub folder now, while well — one emergency plan per course that a stranger can run. The main reason people teach sick is that staying home costs more work than going in. Remove that cost in advance.
4Days two and threeC
The peak. The goal is to not make it worse and to not make it longer.
Eat — for a viral illness, eating is the side the evidence leans towards (see “feed a cold” in the folklore section). Don’t force food if you have no appetite, but don’t fast on purpose.
Drink to thirst, plus a bit — there are no trials showing extra fluids shorten a cold, but fever, a dry indoor atmosphere and mouth-breathing all cost water. Warm drinks earn their place for comfort alone.
Honey for the cough and the rest of the relief table below, matched to the symptom you actually have.
Sleep as much as the body asks — the sleepiness is part of the immune response, not laziness.

Relief, by symptomNothing here cures a cold. These make it more bearable, and two of them may make it shorter. Match the remedy to the symptom you have instead of taking one of everything.
Cough
HoneyBWORKS

A 2021 systematic review (Abuelgasim, Oxford) of 14 trials found honey beat usual care for cough frequency, cough severity and overall symptoms. It outperformed diphenhydramine and did about as well as dextromethorphan.

Use: a spoonful or two straight or in a warm drink, as needed, especially before bed. Never give honey to a baby under one.
Dextromethorphan (the “DM” syrups)CMARGINAL

Trials of over-the-counter cough medicines for colds are mixed and mostly small. Honey is the better first move.

Blocked or running nose
Salt-water rinse and gargleBPROMISING

In the Edinburgh ELVIS pilot trial (Ramalingam, 2019), 66 adults with colds who did hypertonic saline nasal irrigation and gargling recovered about two days sooner, used fewer medicines, and passed the cold to fewer household members. The 2024 ELVIS-Kids trial of about 300 children with colds found salt-water nose drops cut symptoms from about eight days to six. Neither trial was blinded.

Use: a squeeze bottle or neti pot with saline, several times a day while symptoms last, plus a salt-water gargle. Only ever with distilled, sterile, or boiled-then-cooled water — see the neti-pot entry in the folklore section for why.
Pseudoephedrine (behind the pharmacy counter)BWORKS, MODESTLY

A real oral decongestant with a small, genuine benefit for congestion in adults. It can raise blood pressure and heart rate and disturb sleep, so it is a pharmacist conversation if you have blood pressure issues or take other medications.

Oral phenylephrine (the “PE” products)ADOESN’T WORK

The US FDA’s advisory committee voted unanimously in 2023 that oral phenylephrine does not work as a decongestant, and in 2024 the FDA proposed removing it; less than 1% of the swallowed dose reaches the bloodstream. Many cold-and-flu combination products still contain it. Check the label.

Decongestant sprays (oxymetazoline, xylometazoline)BSHORT TERM ONLY

Fast and effective, but more than about three days of use causes rebound congestion that is worse than what you started with.

Antihistamines aloneBLITTLE BENEFIT

For a cold, on their own, a small short-lived effect on sneezing and not much else. The drowsy ones are a sleep aid, not a cold treatment.

Sore throat, aches, fever
Ibuprofen or acetaminophenAWORKS

Both reliably ease headache, sore throat, muscle aches and fever. They do nothing for the runny nose or cough.

Use: for comfort, not to chase a number on the thermometer. Fever is part of the defence, and a couple of older cold-virus studies linked aspirin and acetaminophen to a weaker antibody response and, for aspirin, more virus shedding. Small studies, graded C. If the fever is making you miserable or stopping sleep, treat it.
Combination “cold and flu” productsBCAREFUL

Combinations of a painkiller, a decongestant and an antihistamine give a small overall benefit in trials. The real risk is stacking: many contain acetaminophen, and taking a combination product plus plain acetaminophen is how people overshoot the daily maximum without noticing. Read every label and add up the acetaminophen.

Hot drinksCFEELS BETTER

A small Cardiff study (Sanu and Eccles, 2008) found a hot drink gave immediate, sustained relief of runny nose, cough, sneezing, sore throat and tiredness; the same drink at room temperature helped only some symptoms. Comfort is a legitimate outcome.

Steam and humidified airBNO CLEAR BENEFIT

A Cochrane review of heated humidified air for colds found no consistent benefit. It won’t hurt, but don’t expect much — and never lean over a pot of boiling water, which is a common way to get scalded.

Menthol chest rubsCSUBJECTIVE

Menthol makes airflow feel easier without measurably changing it. In one trial with children, a vapour rub improved parents’ ratings of sleep and cough. Chest and neck only; never in or under the nose.

AntibioticsAWRONG TOOL

Colds and flu are viral; antibiotics do nothing against them, cause side effects, and breed resistance. They matter only when a bacterial complication develops — which is a doctor’s call, and one of the red flags below.

The supplement ledgerWhat the aisle promises against what the trials found. The honest summary: one supplement has a real case at the onset of a cold, one fixes a deficiency, and most of the rest are unproven either way.
Zinc lozengesBWORTH IT, AT ONSET

The best-supported item on the shelf, but only in a specific form: acetate or gluconate lozenges, dissolved slowly, over 75 mg elemental zinc per day, started within 24 hours. Low-dose lozenges showed no effect. Hemilä’s analysis of seven high-dose trials found colds about 33% shorter; the 2024 Cochrane review rated the evidence low certainty.

Watch for: bad taste and nausea are common. Avoid lozenges containing citric acid, which binds zinc. Never use zinc nasal sprays or gels — they have been linked to permanent loss of smell. Short courses only; months of high-dose zinc can deplete copper.
Vitamin CADAILY, NOT RESCUE

The Cochrane review (Hemilä and Chalker, 2013) is clear on three points. Taken every day, at least 200 mg shortened colds in adults by about 8%. It did not reduce how many colds ordinary people caught. And starting high doses after symptoms began showed no consistent effect. The exception: in marathon runners, skiers and soldiers in subarctic exercise, it halved cold risk.

Verdict: a fruit-and-vegetable diet gets most people there. Megadosing at the first sneeze is not supported.
Vitamin DBFIX A DEFICIENCY

The 2021 meta-analysis (Jolliffe, Martineau) found a small protective effect, strongest with daily doses of 400–1,000 IU, with big weekly or monthly doses not working. The 2025 update, adding a very large new trial, gave a similar estimate but no longer statistically significant.

Verdict: at Richmond’s latitude the winter sun is too weak to make much vitamin D, so a modest daily dose through the dark months is reasonable, and a blood test settles whether you are low. It is not a cold remedy.
ProbioticsCMAYBE, FOR PREVENTION

Cochrane (2022, 23 trials, about 7,000 people) found probiotics better than placebo at preventing upper-respiratory infections, with low-to-moderate certainty and many different strains and doses. Side effects were minor and digestive.

EchinaceaCUNPROVEN

Cochrane (2014, 24 trials) found no significant prevention, while not ruling out a small effect. Products vary so much in species, plant part and extraction that “echinacea” is barely one thing. Allergy risk if you react to ragweed or daisies.

ElderberryCUNPROVEN

A 2021 systematic review (Wieland) found only five trials. Elderberry probably doesn’t prevent colds; it may shorten colds and flu, but the evidence is uncertain. No evidence it overstimulates the immune system. Raw berries, leaves and stems are toxic — commercial syrups only.

GarlicCUNPROVEN

The Cochrane review found a single eligible trial of 146 people with fewer colds on garlic. One trial is a hint, not an answer. Fine as food.

Andrographis and pelargoniumCWEAK SIGNAL

Both herbal extracts have trials suggesting some symptom relief — andrographis for cough and sore throat, pelargonium (sold as Umcka) mainly for acute bronchitis — but most trials are small and low quality. Both carry side-effect reports, including digestive upset and allergy, and pelargonium has case reports of liver injury.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC)CUNPROVEN

One Italian trial in older adults found fewer symptomatic flu-like episodes over a winter. Not replicated. No case for it at the onset of a cold.

Colloidal silverDHARMFUL

No evidence of benefit taken by mouth. Long-term use can cause argyria, a permanent blue-grey discolouration of the skin, and it interferes with some antibiotics and thyroid medication.

Folklore on trialGrandmothers were right more often than the internet gives them credit for — and wrong in some specific, checkable ways.
“Chicken soup cures a cold”CPARTLY TRUE

Not a cure. A 2000 lab study (Rennard) found chicken soup slowed the movement of inflammatory white cells in a dish, a possible mild anti-inflammatory effect. The rest of its value is solid: warm fluid, salt, calories, and steam that feels good. As a vehicle for eating when sick, it is excellent.

“Feed a cold, starve a fever”CPARTLY TRUE

A 2016 Yale study (Wang, Medzhitov) found that in mice with flu, feeding, specifically glucose, improved survival, while in mice with a bacterial infection, feeding killed them. That fits the proverb surprisingly well, but it was in mice. In people: eat when you have a viral illness, and don’t force food when you have none. Whether a fever is viral or bacterial is often not something you can tell at home.

“Go out with wet hair and you’ll catch a cold”CPARTLY TRUE

Cold does not create a virus, and older virus-challenge studies found chilling did not make volunteers easier to infect. But a Cardiff trial (Johnson and Eccles, 2005) had 90 people sit with their feet in cold water for 20 minutes: 13 reported a cold in the following days, against 5 of 90 controls. The theory is that chilling tips an infection you were already quietly carrying into symptoms. The trial was unblinded and relied on self-report. Add the 2022 nasal-temperature work, and “wrap up” is reasonable — for the virus you already caught.

“Dairy makes more mucus”BMYTH

In a study that infected volunteers with a cold virus and weighed their nasal secretions (Pinnock, 1990), milk intake was not linked to mucus production. Milk can leave a coating in the mouth that feels like phlegm. If you like milk, there is no reason to drop it while sick.

“A hot toddy”CPARTLY TRUE

The hot water, lemon and honey help. The whisky doesn’t: alcohol fragments sleep and nudges dehydration, which are the two things you need least. Make it without the spirit and keep the good half.

“Sweat it out”DMYTH

No evidence that deliberate sweating clears a virus. With a fever, a sauna or heavy layering adds heat and fluid loss to a body already struggling with both. The Sauna protocol is for healthy days.

“Vicks on the soles of your feet”DMYTH

Whatever vapour rubs do, they do by being smelled. On the feet, under socks, there is no route by which it could affect a cough. Any effect is the menthol drifting up, or the ritual.

“Sliced onion in your socks”DMYTH

No trials and no plausible mechanism. Onions do not draw toxins or viruses out through the feet. Harmless, pointless, and your socks will smell of onion.

“Green snot means you need antibiotics”BMYTH

The colour comes from an enzyme in white blood cells, which turn up for viral infections too. Studies of patients with cough found sputum colour a poor guide to whether bacteria were involved. What matters is the pattern over time, not the colour on a given day.

“Antibiotics will knock it out”AHARMFUL

No effect on viruses; real side effects; contributes to resistance. See Relief.

“The flu shot gives you the flu”AMYTH

The injected vaccine has no live virus and cannot cause flu. Feeling achy for a day is the immune system responding. Catching something in the fortnight before protection builds, or catching a different virus entirely, gets blamed on the shot.

“You’re only contagious with a fever”AMYTH

Flu spreads from the day before symptoms; colds spread easily with no fever at all. Feeling fine is not proof of not spreading it.

“A cold can turn into the flu”AMYTH

Different viruses; one cannot become the other. What does happen is a secondary bacterial infection — sinus, ear, chest — after a cold. Its signature is getting better and then getting worse, which is on the red-flag list.

“Megadose vitamin C the moment it starts”AMYTH

Trials that started vitamin C after symptoms began showed no consistent effect. See the ledger.

“Gargle with salt water”BSUPPORTED

Soothing for a sore throat, and part of the saline routine that shortened colds in the ELVIS trials. Half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water.

“Honey for a cough”BSUPPORTED

One of the few remedies that beat usual care head to head. See Relief.

“Go to bed and sleep it off”BSUPPORTED

Sleep is tied to immune function in both directions, and the sleepiness of illness is part of the response. The old advice is sound.

“Work out and burn it off”DPARTLY TRUE

The “neck check” used by sports medicine: symptoms only above the neck — runny nose, mild sore throat — and light-to-moderate exercise is usually fine if you feel up to it. Fever, body aches, chest symptoms or gut symptoms mean no exercise, because viral illness can inflame the heart muscle and hard exertion then is a known risk. This is expert consensus, not trial evidence. Exercise does not shorten a cold.

Ginger shots, apple cider vinegar, oil of oreganoDUNPROVEN

Each has lab studies showing some antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory activity, and none has good human trials for colds. In food amounts they are harmless. Concentrated oregano oil can burn the mouth and upset the stomach, and undiluted vinegar can damage teeth and throat.

“Rinse your nose with tap water”AHARMFUL

Rare but real: tap water can carry organisms, including the amoeba Naegleria fowleri, that are harmless to drink but can cause a fatal brain infection when flushed up the nose. Rinses use distilled or sterile water, or water boiled for a minute and cooled. Clean and dry the bottle after every use.

Red flagsMost colds and flu resolve on their own. These are the patterns that mean it has stopped being a home-care problem.
Call 9-1-1
Struggling to breathe at rest, or lips turning blue or grey. Chest pain or pressure. Confusion, being hard to wake, or a seizure. A high fever with a stiff neck, or with a rash that doesn’t fade when pressed under a glass.
Call 8-1-1 or see a doctor the same day
Better, then worse — improving for a few days, then fever returns or symptoms jump; the classic sign of a secondary bacterial infection. A fever lasting more than three days. A severe sore throat with fever but no cough or runny nose (a strep pattern). Facial pain and thick discharge beyond ten days. Ear pain. Can’t keep fluids down, or very little urine. Shortness of breath when you move that you didn’t have before. And any flu or COVID if you are in a higher-risk group, early, because of the antiviral windows.
A cough that lingers for up to three weeks after a cold is common and usually not a red flag on its own. Past three weeks, get it checked.

Coming backThe most common way to lose a second week is to return at full speed on day three.
Back to the classroomB
The rule — 24 hours without fever, without fever reducers, and symptoms improving overall.
The next five days — you can still be shedding virus. Current guidance suggests extra care: a mask if coughing, hands after every cough, a little more distance from students’ desks, windows open.
Pace the first day — teach the plan the sub left off, don’t try to recover lost ground in one lesson.
Back to trainingD
Expert practice rather than trials, but consistent across sports medicine.
Above-the-neck cold — light sessions are fine if you feel up to them. Stop if you feel worse.
After a fever or flu — wait until you are fever-free and the aches are gone, then come back at about half volume and intensity for the first session and build over a week.
Contact and sparring last — Krav is close-contact and breath-heavy, which makes it both the hardest session to do sick and the easiest place to pass it on. Drills before sparring.
Stop signs — chest pain, palpitations, unusual breathlessness, or feeling faint during a comeback session. Stop and get checked; after a viral illness these are not “push through” symptoms.
Repay the debtC
Sleep — the week after an illness is not the week to go short. The recovery and the next exposure are both waiting.
Restock the kit — replace what you used while you still remember what ran out.

The one thing
Treat the first tickle as a deadline.
Zinc and salt water the same day, the hard session cancelled, an early night. Almost everything that shortens an illness works only in the first 24 hours, and the most common mistake is spending those hours deciding whether you are really getting sick. Upstream of that, the one prevention move that outranks the rest is the one you already track: seven hours, especially in the weeks the building fills up.
Failure modes
Waiting to see — by the time it is clearly a cold, the zinc window is gone.
Teaching through a fever on ibuprofen — you are contagious and the drug is hiding the stay-home signal.
Stacking combination products — two sources of acetaminophen, one of which you forgot was in the cold medicine.
Buying the aisle instead of the sleep — five supplements and a 5.5-hour night is the wrong trade.
Full intensity on day three — the relapse that turns one week into two.
Paying for oral phenylephrine — the most common decongestant on the shelf, and the one that doesn’t work.

Shaping and Adapting

After Maj. Paul Tremblay Jr, Shaping and Adapting: Unlocking the Power of Colonel John Boyd’s OODA Loop (USMC Command and Staff College, 2015). Stay matched to reality. Speed only counts when the picture is right.

The idea
The loop you were taught is wrongC
The familiar picture is four arrows in a circle: observe, orient, decide, act, and whoever cycles fastest wins. Boyd never drew it that way. His final 1993 diagram is a web, and the hub is Orientation: your heritage, your culture, your experience, new information, and the constant breaking-down and rebuilding of your mental models.
Boyd’s 1993 diagram, redrawn top to bottom for a phone. Teal: feedback. Gold: implicit guidance and control.
Orientation decides what you notice. It is not just the lens you interpret with; it filters what reaches you in the first place. A flawed orientation doesn’t feel flawed. It feels like seeing.
A decision is a hypothesis. An action is a test. Boyd wrote those words on the diagram. You act partly to find out whether your picture was right.
The gold lines are intuition. A trained orientation drives action directly, skipping the decision step. That is the expert’s speed. With a thin orientation the same shortcut produces fast, scripted action on cues that aren’t there.
Relative speed, not absolute. The advantage goes to whoever stays matched to reality while the situation moves. Fast action on a wrong picture is just falling into the trap sooner.
The failure to watch for: incestuous amplification. Your expectations shape what you observe, the distorted observations confirm the expectations, and the loop closes on itself. Chuck Spinney compared it to a microphone held next to its own speaker. Early on the errors are small and hidden by luck, which is why it feels fine from inside. The tell: nothing surprises you, every signal agrees with you, and disagreement gets explained away. The cure is structural: open the loop to the world. Three of the six moves below exist to do exactly that.
The seven moves
The relevance gateC
Before you start something new, name the outside observation that says it’s needed now. No observation, no start.
Use when an idea arrives for a new build, task, project, purchase or routine, especially one that feels urgent and exciting. Not for three-second decisions, and never for time with the girls.
Name the signal in one sentence — something you saw, were told, or measured. Something you thought is not a signal.
No signal: park it. It goes to the parked list and waits for Sunday. That is the three-tier rule with a sharper test attached.
Weak signal: test before you build. Take the cheapest action that would produce a real signal, not the full version.
In practice
Room 148Before building a new worksheet variant, point to the student work that shows the current one isn’t working.“Three students left Part C blank on two sheets” passes. “It could be better” doesn’t.
Forge OSBefore any new install, name the thing that broke or the number that stalled.A card you’ve stopped reading is a signal to remove one, not add one. “It would be neat” goes to the parked list.
IncomeBefore a new income idea, name one person who said they’d pay or one number that moved.Without that, it queues behind the Forge course, which already has a signal to chase: pilot users.
HealthBefore adding a supplement, protocol or tracker, name the reading that says the current one isn’t working.“My sleep average has dropped three weeks running” passes. A podcast recommendation is outside information worth noting, not yet a reason.
HomeWhen a plan for the girls occurs to you mid-evening, check whether it came from something she said or did.If it came from your head, the relevant action is usually to spend the evening with her rather than design for her.
Failure signal: every idea finds a signal. Ask whether you’d have noticed that signal before you had the idea. If not, you went looking for permission.
The outside judgeAB
Everything you’re building gets a judge that isn’t you. Where you’re the only evaluator, you’re grading your own picture of the world.
Use when once now for every live project, then whenever something new becomes live.
The one-time sort (15 minutes). List each live project. Beside it, write its external signal and when you’ll next see it.
No judge: add one this week, or accept the project is private practice rather than a bet, and stop evaluating it as one.
When the judge speaks, it outranks the feeling — until another outside signal says otherwise.
In practice
Room 148The judge is exit tickets and student work, not how the lesson felt.After each unit, write your gut read of how it went, then look at the tickets. Note where the two disagree; that gap is your orientation error.
FLA 6/7Adults will tell you directly if asked. Mid-term, ask two questions on paper: what helped most, and what wasted your time.Anonymous, so the answers aren’t shaped by who’s asking.
IncomeForge course: pilot users before another rewrite. POF book: one reader who will push back. Stock system: the forward test, never the backtest.A backtest is the model grading itself on data it was built from. It is the purest example of a closed loop.
HealthThe judge is the data: sleep score, a lift or pace number, your Krav partner’s read of your sparring.Not how disciplined you felt this week.
HomeThe girls are the judge of your fathering, not your checklist.Every so often ask what the best part of their week was. If you never feature, that’s data too.
Failure signal: a judge that has never surprised you. Either you picked one who agrees with you, or you aren’t hearing it. Find another.
Write the predictionB
A decision is a hypothesis. Write down the result you expect before you act, so the action becomes a test that teaches you something.
Use when an action is consequential: a new unit, a routine change, a system rule, a hard conversation. Not on three-second decisions.
One line: “I expect X by Y.” Specific enough to be wrong. “They’ll improve” can’t miss, so it can’t teach.
Add a confidence word if you like — likely, coin flip, or long shot.
On Sunday, mark each one: hit, miss, or can’t tell. Look for what the misses have in common, not at the score.
In practice
Room 148Predict each unit’s outcome on its first day.“By Friday, most of ELL 3 will use the target structure unprompted on the exit ticket.”
IncomePredict before each launch or send.“Two of five pilot users will finish module one within a week.” If it’s zero, the misses tell you where the course loses people.
Stock systemBefore overriding a system signal by hand, write what you expect and why.Your overrides then become a record of whether your judgement beats the model. Right now you don’t know.
HealthPredict the effect of any change before you make it.“Moving bedtime 30 minutes earlier lifts my weekly sleep average within two weeks.”
HomePredict how a change of approach will land.“If I ask about her day at dinner instead of in the car, I’ll get more than one-word answers.”
FantasyUse lineup and waiver calls as free calibration reps.Low stakes, weekly feedback, clear outcomes: an ideal practice ground for judging your own confidence.
Failure signal: the score becomes a verdict on you. A miss is information about your picture, not your worth. One honest mark, then move on.
Re-orient on new groundB
When the ground changes, your old picture still feels like perception, but it’s memory. Look for what surprises you.
Use when anything is new: Richmond High this year, a new class or cohort, a new group, a new role, or someone close entering a new stage.
The weekly surprise note, for six weeks. One line: what didn’t match what you expected.
A week with no surprise is the warning. You’re seeing your model, not the room. Look harder or ask someone.
Ask the insider: “What do newcomers here usually get wrong?”
Update on two signals, not one. One surprise is an anecdote; the same surprise twice is a change in the ground.
In practice
Richmond HighFifteen years away from high school means your model of teenagers was built somewhere else. Keep the surprise note through October.Watch what students do in the first five minutes of class before you correct anything. Ask a teacher who has been there for years what newcomers get wrong.
Block BEach host teacher’s room is their ground, not yours.Before offering support, ask each one what kind of help actually helps in their room, and watch one lesson before changing anything.
FLA 6/7Every new term is a new cohort. Treat the first night as observation.Last term’s first-night plan was built for last term’s people.
HomeYour daughters keep moving to new ground.What your older daughter wanted two years ago may not be what she wants now. Ask rather than rerun the old plan.
TravelSomewhere new, run the surprise note daily for the first few days.It turns being disoriented into something useful.
Failure signal: explaining every surprise away as an exception. If they all turn out to be exceptions, the rule you’re protecting is the thing that’s out of date.
Find the contradictionsABC
Boyd’s moral design starts at home: avoid mismatches between “what we say we are, what we are, and the world we have to deal with.” Find the gaps on purpose, then close them one at a time.
Use when Sunday, for the audit; any time a reaction is sharper than the moment deserved; or when someone who doesn’t share your assumptions throws you. Never in the middle of a lesson or an evening.
Find them
Start from claims you already hold. The list is your Fixed Core: the values and hard lines from the Orientation protocol. Not new claims invented for the occasion.
The records audit. Check each claim against something you didn’t write in the moment: the planner, the calendar, the Forge logs, spending, screen time. Where the record and the claim disagree, you have a candidate.
The defensiveness log. A sharp reaction to a mild comment marks a claim that is carrying weight on thin support. Afterwards, note the trigger and the claim it threatened.
Where the script broke. Boyd, citing Edward Hall: you only see your own hidden program when someone doesn’t follow it. When a colleague, a student or a new setting throws you, ask which assumption it exposed.
Other people are the fourth detector — that’s the outside judge. Ask someone who sees you often: “What do I say about myself that you don’t see me do?” Don’t argue with the answer.
Then close them
Decide which of the three is wrong. The behaviour: the claim stands, so what you do changes. Hard lines always go here; a hard line is never relaxed to fit the record. The claim: it was aspirational and the record is honest, so restate it as a goal (“I’m working on…”) or drop it. The world: it was true on old ground, so go to Re-orient on new ground, and often on to Destroy to create.
Fix it as a behaviour, not a better-worded claim. Write it as “When X, I will Y,” and name the record that will show whether it held.
One at a time. The Fixed Core claim with the widest gap goes first. The rest wait for Sunday.
Data, not a verdict. A contradiction is information about your picture. Judging yourself for it starts another evaluating loop, the kind “not relevant” exists to cut.
In practice
HomeClaim: family comes first. Record: a week of the planner and calendar — where the free evening hours actually went.If project time beat the girls most nights, the behaviour changes, not the claim. “On a night I’m home by six, the laptop stays in the bag until they’re in bed.” The record: the planner next Sunday.
IncomeClaim: I’m building income to leave teaching. Record: planner hours on income work against hours on the OS.If the OS wins every week, either the behaviour changes (“When a work block starts, the first 45 minutes go to the course”) or the claim is honestly restated. Keeping the claim and ignoring the hours is the contradiction.
Room 148Claim: every student gets a fair shot. Record: whose names show up in your exit-ticket comments over two weeks.If the same quiet four never appear: “When I open the exit-ticket pile, I start with those four.”
Block BA host teacher’s mild comment stings for the rest of the day.Log it that evening. Which claim did it threaten — “I know how to support ELL students”? Then check that claim against what her students actually produce with your support.
FLA 6/7An adult learner flatly refuses an activity you’ve run for years.That’s the script breaking. Ask which assumption it exposed — perhaps that adults want what teenagers want — before deciding who was wrong.
TrainingClaim: I train five days a week. Record: the last month’s log shows three.Pick one: a trigger that gets you to five, or the honest claim at three. Either closes the gap. The claim plus a quiet log doesn’t.
Forge OSClaim: the system serves the family. Record: builds this month against the evenings they took.If the count runs the wrong way, that’s a finding for this move, not a reason for another build.
Failure signal: the audit only finds contradictions you’re already comfortable admitting, or every one gets resolved by rewording the claim. If the behaviour never changes, you’re editing the record, not closing the gap.
Destroy to createBC
Adapting means breaking old structures down and building new ones from the parts. A system that never ends anything isn’t adapting; it’s accumulating.
Use when the quarterly triage comes round; a project has been paused for months; or a belief keeps producing the same bad result.
Commit in advance. Before the quarterly review, write that you will kill at least one project or one working assumption. Your first triage kept all sixteen projects alive.
Kill it for parts. Mark it dead (it stays visible, as you decided), then name the one piece worth carrying into something live.
Use the clean-slate question: “Would I start this today, knowing what I know now?” What you’ve already spent is not an answer.
Hunt one assumption. Name one belief you act on that you haven’t tested in a year, and turn it into a prediction.
Hunt the anomaly, don’t explain it away. When something doesn’t fit what you believe, that friction is the material — sit with it instead of finding the reading that makes it fit after all.
Import from outside your own field. A new combination rarely comes from going deeper into what you already know. Pull a frame from somewhere unrelated and force it against the problem.
Write the belief down, then argue against it. You can’t destroy an idea you haven’t made explicit. State it plainly, then attack it as your smartest critic would — if it doesn’t cost you something, you steelmanned instead of attacking.
Expect the dip. The first rebuilt version is usually worse than the one you tore down. That’s the cost of the method, not a sign you got it wrong.
Find someone who disagrees on the fundamentals, not the details. Someone quibbling inside your frame won’t show you what you can’t see from inside it.
In practice
ProjectsTake the paused list into the next quarterly review with the clean-slate question already written beside each item.You choose which ones die. The protocol only insists that something does.
Room 148Each unit, retire one activity you keep mainly because it’s familiar.Keep the part that worked and fold it into its replacement.
Forge OSEach quarter, remove one card, key or tracker you no longer read.Every card is upkeep, and upkeep on something you ignore is pure cost.
HealthDrop one routine you do out of habit that has no measured effect.Or turn it into a prediction and give it four weeks to earn its place.
BeliefsTest one belief about what your students can handle, what the girls want from you, or what earns money.Beliefs in these three areas quietly steer the most decisions and get checked the least.
Room 148A lesson keeps landing flat and you keep tweaking the same activity.Stop patching it. Name the belief about what engages them that the activity was built on, and test a different one.
Stock systemA rule you wrote a year ago keeps costing you the same way.Pull a frame from outside trading — how another field handles the same kind of recurring error — before patching the rule again.
BeliefsSomething a colleague, your daughter, or the market does keeps surprising you.That’s the anomaly. Write down what you actually believe there, then argue the other side before revising it.
Failure signal: killing something you rebuild a month later, the kill list turning into its own project, or every rebuild landing back at your first draft because nothing outside your own head ever pushed on it. One kill, cleanly, then back to work.
Engineer the endingsA
Memory is weighted by the high point and the ending, and memory is what orientation is built from. Choose how things end.
Use when something is closing: the last minutes of a lesson, an evening, a conversation, a week, a term, a workout.
Plan the last five minutes, not just the first.
End on something that worked, not on what’s left over.
When you review, correct for your own memory. Judge the whole stretch, not how it finished.
In practice
Room 148Protect the exit ticket and a closing line naming what students can now do that they couldn’t at the start.Never let the bell land mid-instruction; that becomes the lesson they remember.
FLA 6/7Close on the night’s success first, then outstanding assignments.Adults who come after a working day decide whether to come back partly on how the last class ended.
HomeMake the last words at drop-off and bedtime warm, never logistics or a correction.“Clean your room” can be said at 6 p.m. It shouldn’t be what she falls asleep on.
Hard talksEnd on one concrete next step and a sign that the relationship is fine.The ending decides whether she brings the next problem to you.
TrainingEnd sessions on a clean rep or a round you won.You’ll remember the session as better and turn up for the next one more readily.
SundayBefore judging the week, name one good thing from Monday or Tuesday.It counters the pull to grade the week on Friday.
Failure signal: using a good ending to cover a real problem. Engineer the ending; don’t edit the record.

The Sunday block
Four questionsin the review you already run
Which predictions missed, and what do the misses have in common?
Did any outside judge disagree with me this week, and what did I do about it?
What surprised me? If nothing did, where wasn’t I looking?
Which claim did the record disagree with this week, and does last week’s fix show up in the record?
Together they check that the loop is still open. Nothing new to track.
The one thing
Who, other than me, says this is working?
Give everything that matters an outside judge, and believe the judge over the feeling. It is the one move that keeps the loop open when every other part of you would rather it stayed closed.
Failure modes
Building machinery instead of acting — turning this page into new trackers is orientation work used to avoid action, which is the exact failure the paper warns about. The Sunday block lives in the review you already run.
Gating everything — the relevance gate is for new loops and optimizing. Three-second decisions just get made, and time with the girls never needs a reason.
Running it live — predictions, surprise notes and judges belong before and after. In the middle of a lesson or an evening, attention goes on the room.
Chasing speed — reading Boyd as “decide faster” is the misreading this paper was written to correct.
The audit as a weapon — finding contradictions to prove you’re a fraud rather than to fix one. One gap, one behaviour, then back to work.
Friendly judges — picking evaluators who will agree with you, then calling the loop open.
This page as the alibi — reading about shaping and adapting feels like doing it. It’s worth something only if a judge, a prediction or a kill exists by next Sunday.
Evidence
A Strong. Peak-end effect: remembered experience is dominated by its high point and its ending (Kahneman et al. 1993; Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996). Confirmation bias: people seek and weight evidence that fits what they already believe (Nickerson 1998 review). The mechanism behind incestuous amplification and the case for outside judges. Implementation intentions: “when X, I will Y” plans substantially raise follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis) — why a contradiction is fixed as a trigger, not a better claim.

B Good, with variance. Intuition is only trustworthy in regular environments with fast, clear feedback (Kahneman & Klein 2009) — the case for supplying feedback where it’s missing, and for distrusting old intuitions on new ground. Recognition-primed decisions: experts match patterns and test the first workable option (Klein, Sources of Power). Writing and scoring predictions improves forecasting accuracy (Tetlock’s Good Judgment research; Mellers et al. 2014). Sunk-cost effect: past investment keeps failing projects alive (Arkes & Blumer 1985). Intentions predict behaviour only loosely (Webb & Sheeran 2006), and self-reports drift from measured records — for example, self-reported exercise versus measured activity (Prince et al. 2008). The case for auditing claims against records rather than memory.
